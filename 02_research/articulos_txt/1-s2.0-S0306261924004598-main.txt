Three stage metal hydride hydrogen separation system was tested, wherein each stage was configured with 1.2
kg of LaNi4.7Al0.3, LaNi5 and La0.9Ce0.1Ni5, placed in descending order of their equilibrium bed pressure
respectively as R1 (La0.9Ce0.1Ni5) > R2 (LaNi5) > R3 (LaNi4.7Al0.3). Hydrogen absorption capacity obtained for
R1, R2 and R3 were 15.91 g, 15.93 g and 16.02 g respectively for 3rd activation cycle, performed at 30 bar and
25 ◦ C. For coupled reactor operation, optimum hydrogen transfer was for desorbing reactor at 90 ◦ C and
absorbing reactor at 20 ◦ C, which was 12.31 g for R1 to R2 in 350 s, 13.33 g for R2 to R3 in 300 s and 13.38 g for
R1 to R2 in 280 s, constituting to 1.03, 1.11 and 1.12 wt% of hydrogen storage respectively. Further, the system
was tested for hydrogen purification, wherein inlet sample with 80% impurity (18% Ar, 20% CO2, 20% N2, 20%
CH4 and 2% CO) and 20% H2 was used for purification study. According to Thermal Conductivity Detector (TCD)
results, the system delivered 91.54%, 99.996% and 99.9999% pure hydrogen respectively for stage 1, stage 2 and
stage 3 purification. The system works within low temperature range of 20–90 ◦ C.

Observing consistent rise in global warming, due to dependency on
fossil fuels to meet global energy demand, the world is looking for a
parallel shift towards the renewable energy utilization technologies to
meet energy demand without affecting environment [1–3]. Among
different renewable energy sources, hydrogen stands firmly due to its
high energy density, and capabilities to replace conventional energy
sources effectively [4,5]. As an excellent energy carrier and environ­
mental friendly in nature, H2 has become perfect option for limiting the
rapid increase in CO2 concentration in the atmosphere and restricting
global temperature rise to 1.5 ◦ C. Therefore, the world is gearing to­
wards hydrogen energy economy for fulfilling the various energy de­
mand [6,7]. Hydrogen can be extracted through electrolysis of water, PV
electrolysis, decomposition of fossil fuels, thermolysis of water,
reforming, biomass conversion, gasification, dark fermentation, bio
photolysis, etc. [8–11]. And, it can be utilised for different engineering/
industrial applications such as hydrogen fuel cell, food and chemical
industries, automobile industries, ceramic and steel industries, weather
balloon, synthesis of chemical compounds, space sector, etc. [12–16].
However, most of the engineering application requires pure grade
hydrogen for its efficient operation. One of such examples is the use of
hydrogen in electric generator coil cooling, wherein the performance of
generator in the thermal power plants is directly influenced by the pu­
rity level of hydrogen [17,18]. Similarly, efficiency of hydrogen fuel cell

gaseous impurities remains in the void volume. Absorption is followed
by flushing, which is performed at low temperature and the impure
gases present in the void space is released to the atmosphere. In the last
stage i.e. desorption, the temperature of MH bed is raised to elevated
temperature, and pure hydrogen is desorbed as an end product.

However, some metal hydrides fall under the group of AB5, A2B and AB2
are suitable for hydrogen purification application [19,35].
In 1996, Au et al. [36] reported the used of metal hydride (MlNi5xAlx) for storing, purifying, recovering, and transporting hydrogen from
a purge gas line of an ammonia plant. The system reported hydrogen
recovery of around 70% with up to 97% saving in power consumption,
which resulted to 60% saving in hydrogen price. According to Dunikov
et al. [37], N2 hardy affects purification characteristics of AB5, but it
decreases storage capacity by 3–4%. A test was performed with 27% N2
in H2 + N2 gas mixture, which was fed to LaFe0.1Mn0.3Ni4.8. Hydrogen
loss of <5% was observed during the recovery phase. However, impu­
rities such as CO, CO2 and H2S significantly affect rate of reaction by
slowing down initial rate of reactor by 2 to 20 times [38,39]. According
to Oztek M.T [40], impurities like He and Ar have no impact on the
reversible purification characteristics of alloys like Mg2Ni, VTiNi and
LaNi5. However, the impurities like CO2, CO, O2, H2S can cause alloy
poisoning. Handa et al. [41] reported poisoning characteristics of CO2
on various AB5 alloys. The poisoning characteristics of CO2 on various
AB5 alloys, varied in the sequence CaNi5 < LaNi5 < MmNi4.025Co0.4M­
n0.275Al0.3 < LaCo5 respectively. Compared to other gaseous impurities,
the poisoning effect of CO was comparatively more dominant. Wan et al.
[42] reported poisoning characteristics of CO on LaNi4⋅7Al0⋅3, wherein
H2 with 300 ppm CO content was used for the study. Significant
poisoning was observed, and the alloy got deactivated after 3 cycle,
wherein absorption was carried out at 30 ◦ C. But the storage capacity
was not affected, when tested at 80 ◦ C. However, the poisoned alloy was

regenerated/reactivated after one pure hydrogen absorption cycle at
high pressure.
Use of intermetallic alloys for bio hydrogen purification and storage

multiple purification stages [46,47]. Kazakov et al. [46] reported the use
of LaNi4.4Fe0.3Al0.3 and LaNi4.6Mn0.2Al0.2 as hydrogen purification
alloy, to separate hydrogen from gas sample of biological hydrogen
production stream. They reported that the alloys were suitable for
hydrogen separation from a dilute gas stream from biohydrogen pro­

each) in each purification step. The purified hydrogen was sent to PEM
fuel cell, and it was observed that, the degree of purification was suffi­

2.1. Alloy and reactor details
Initially, experiments on the coupled reactor system was performed,
wherein three identical reactors with 6 embedded cooling tubes (ECT)
were filled with different AB5 alloys and arranged as multiple hydrogen
purification batteries/bed in series. The pictorial view of the 6 ECT
reactor with its detailed specification and cooling tube arrangement is
depicted in Fig. 3. The basic configuration of the reactor is similar to that
of shell and tube type heat exchanger, wherein the shell side MH alloy is
placed, and the ECT's facilitates the effective heat transfer to/from the
MH bed. Stainless steel sintered filter of 2 μm pore size is welded at the
centre of the reactor to facilitate uniform hydrogen absorption/
desorption process along the axial direction, keeping alloy in tacked
within the reactor shell. These alloys were arranged in the descending
order of their equilibrium bed pressure at room temperature (PCT
characteristics), such that ‘stage 1 reactor (R1)’ was filled with
La0.9Ce0.1Ni5, ‘stage 2 reactor (R2)’ with LaNi5 and ‘stage 3 reactor (R3)’
with LaNi4.7Al0.3 and these reactors were filled with equal alloy mass of
1.2 kg each. The reason for arranging the metal hydride alloys in its
descending order of PCT characteristics is because of purification
application. Considering the operational temperature and pressure
ranges, the descending order (PCT) of material arrangement, facilitates
maximum hydrogen transfer during coupling, due to higher pressure
gradient for a given temperature range. Parallelly, the last stage alloy,
being more stable as compared with adjacent stages (low equilibrium
pressure), ensures ultrapure hydrogen delivery because of efficient
flushing.
Initially the reactors and the gas circuit were tested at 100 bar
pressure, to ensure the leak proof in gas circuit. The leak test was per­
formed using argon gas. Before conducting coupled system studies,
activation of intermetallic alloys was performed by conducting succes­
sive hydrogenation/dehydrogenation cycles for all the reactor beds. The
activation was carried out using pure hydrogen, which was supplied at
30 bar, wherein the absorption temperature (HTF inlet) of 25 ◦ C was
maintained. The absorption half cycle was followed by the desorption
half cycle which was performed at 90 ◦ C, and the reactor was further
evacuated down to 10− 2 mbar, before the next cycle. The reactors were
reached their maximum absorption capacities and got activated in the
third activation cycle, wherein R1, R2 and R3 absorbed 15.91 g, 15.93 g
and 16.02 g respectively in 201 s, 178 s and 130 s (Fig. 4a).
The given absorption amount contributes to 1.32 wt%, 1.33 wt% and
1.34 wt% of the hydrogen storage capacity for respective reactors. The
corresponding peak bed temperature achieved for each of R1, R2 and R3
were 87.8 ◦ C, 92 ◦ C and 130.5 ◦ C respectively in 16th s, 11th s, and 8th s.
As depicted in Fig. 4b, their respective HTF outlet temperature was
31.4 ◦ C in 22 s, 31.7 ◦ C in 21 s and 35.5 ◦ C in 22 s, for 25 ◦ C absorption
temperature and 30 bar H2 supply pressure. During studies, water as
HTF at fixed flow rate of 4 lpm was used. The uncertainty in experi­
mental measurements was calculated using method adopted by Kline
and McClintock [54]. The uncertainty in measurement was ±2.4%,
±0.5 ◦ C and ± 2% respectively for hydrogen storage, temperature and
pressure measurement. The impure hydrogen mixture was prepared
using Coriolis mass flow meter, which had flow accuracy of ±0.25%.

Though hydrogen purification using intermetallic alloys is a known
technology, however obtaining ultrapure hydrogen (99.9995%) from a
sample with higher impurity range (50–80%) using low grade heat input
(80–90 ◦ C) is still a challenge. There is a significant research gap in
exploring the right combination of metal hydride alloys for hydrogen
purification from higher level of impurity. Considering the scope of
research, a sustainable multistage thermally driven metal hydride based
hydrogen purification system was studied and reported in the present
work. The choice of multistage purification technique for the present
study facilitates the system ability to handle higher range of impurity
content in the supply sample. The MH alloys are selected based on their
thermodynamic properties, such that the system can work in low tem­
perature and pressure range. The consideration of low working tem­
perature allows futuristic approach for its coupling with waste heat
recovery or solar thermal systems having low grade heat input (up to
90 ◦ C). Moreover, the multistage purification technique will facilitate
the recovery of ultrapure hydrogen from the gas samples with even
higher level of impurity [53].
2. Methodology
Three identical MH reactors with 6 embedded cooling tube (ECT)
were fabricated using SS316 material and each were filled with 1.2 kg
La0.9Ce0.1Ni5, LaNi5, and LaNi4.7Al0.3 in the shell space of each reactor,
and were placed in first, second and third purification stages, respec­
tively. Water as HTF was used, that flows through ECT of the reactor,
facilitating effective heat transfer within the reactor. In order to obtain
suitable parameters for the MHHPS operation, parametric studies on
coupled reactor system was performed by varying absorption and
desorption parameters, which is depicted in Table 1, wherein pure
hydrogen was used for study. A complete one cycle of H2 separation
process consists of three steps i.e. absorption of gas mixture, flushing of
impure void gases and desorption of H2 absorbed. The suitable operating
parameters for the absorption, flushing and desorption were obtained
through parametric studies.
During absorption, reactor was maintained at low temperature
(20 ◦ C) and the impure gas mixture was sent through the gas circuit to
the reactor at 20 bar. During absorption, H2 gets absorbed in the inter­
metallic alloys forming hydride (solid solution), whereas the impurity
remains in the void space. Due to exothermic absorption, HTF recircu­
lation at room temperature is facilitated through ECT of the reactor, to
remove the exothermic heat of reaction, from the MH bed to ensure
faster absorption kinetics. Further, the reactor is flushed at low tem­
perature (⁓15 ◦ C) to ensure removal of impurity without losing
hydrogen. Further, reactor is prepared for desorption, wherein it is
raised to 90 ◦ C through continuous HTF recirculation through high
temperature recirculating bath. Once the reactor attains the desired
temperature, the desorption circuit is opened to collect the purified
hydrogen. A Coriolis mass flow meter installed in the gas circuit records
the amount of gas transfer to/from the reactor during absorption,
flushing and desorption. The single and multistage operation of the
purification system is determined by level of impurity present in the gas

2.5. Gas analysis
The gas samples during absorption, desorption and flushing were
collected and analysed using TCD analysis in gas chromatography. CP
Molesieve 5A column was used for the analysis, wherein oven temper­
ature was raised from 40 to 150 ◦ C to facilitate gas separation within the
column. Initially the oven was maintained at 40 ◦ C for 10 min and
further, the temperature of oven vas raised to 150 ◦ C in a time step of
20 ◦ C / min. The retention time of the peak was detected using pure gas
samples of different gases. It was observed that, for column flow rate of
6 lpm, the H2, Ar, N2, CH4 and CO peaks were detected at retention time
of 3-4th min, 5th min, 8-9th min, 11th min and 15-16th min respectively,
considering above oven program. Hydrogen showed a negative peak
because of lower thermal conductivity of He (carrier gas) as compared to
hydrogen. Once peak oven temperature was achieved, it was further
continued for 10 min to facilitate gas detection. The detector tempera­
ture was fixed as 250 ◦ C during the gas analysis. At end of each sample
analysis, the column was further raised to 300 ◦ C, to ensure no gases left
out before the next sample get injected.

2.3. Flushing of void gases
In order to remove unabsorbed impure gases from the void space,
reactors were flushed at low temperature (15 ◦ C). During flushing, the
recirculating chillers were brought down to 15 ◦ C and HTF was recir­
culated through the MH reactor. During recirculation, HTF valve posi­
tioning was same as absorption process, however the gas circuit was set
similar to desorption circuit, wherein, valves Gv5, Gv3 and Gv2 were kept
open and impure gases were directly flushed to atmosphere, or removed
through vacuum pump. The flushing process is completed in a few
seconds (5–10 s), as the void volume was less. For bigger reactor sys­
tems, it may take longer time to flush the reactor. After flushing, the gas
valves were closed, and the reactor was prepared for desorption process.
2.4. Desorption of the absorbed hydrogen
After performing flushing, reactor was prepared for desorption,
wherein HTF at 90 ◦ C was recirculated through R1 using hot recircu­
lating bath. For facilitating HTF recirculation, valves Wv1, Wv2, Wv7 and
Wv9 were kept open, keeping recirculation pump in on position.
Simultaneously, the absorbing reactor R2 was maintained at low tem­
perature (20 ◦ C), through continuous recirculation of HTF using recir­
culating chillers, keeping valves Wv3, Wv4, Wv11 and Wv13 in open
position. Once thermal equilibrium between MH bed and HTF for the
respective absorbing (R2) and desorbing (R1) reactor was maintained,
hydrogen from R1 was sent to R2. During this process, gas valves Gv5,
Gv3 and Gv7 was kept opened to facilitate gas transfer from R1 to R2.
Once, the transfer was completed, R2 was further prepared for flushing
(cooled down to 15 ◦ C), and once the flushing temperature was attained,
the gas valves Gv6, Gv3 and Gv2 were opened to remove void gases.
Further, to obtaining ultra-pure grade of hydrogen (⁓99.9995%
purity), hydrogen transfer from R2 to R3 was performed, wherein R2
was heated to 90 ◦ C and R3 was cooled to 20 ◦ C, by keeping respective
water valves in open position. Once the thermal equilibrium between
the MH bed and HTF was attained, gas valves Gv6, Gv3 and Gv9 were
opened to facilitate H2 transfer from R2 to R3. At end R3 was flushed at
20 ◦ C, similar to flushing in R1 and R2, keeping gas valves, Gv8, Gv3 and
Gv2 in open position and facilitating recirculation of cold HTF through
Wv5, Wv6, Wv12 and Wv14. Finally, the ultra-pure H2 was obtained after
desorption of R3. During desorption, R3 was raised to 90 ◦ C using HTF
recirculation through valves Wv5, Wv6, Wv10 and Wv9. Once the bed
attains thermal equilibrium with HTF, the gas valve Gv8, Gv3 and Gv2
was opened to collect ultra-pure H2. During hydrogen purification pro­
cess, the temperature variation in the MH bed and HTF was recorded

3. Results and discussions
This section presents the experimental outcomes of hydrogen trans­

3.2.1. Stage 1 Purification
Initially, the TCD analysis on the absorption gas sample was con­
ducted and result obtained was calibrated with calibration sample. As
shown in Fig. 8a, the gas composition constitutes of 20% H2, 18% Ar,
20% N2, 20% CH4, 20% CO2 and 2% CO by weight. Lesser CO content in
the impure gas mixture was considered because of its significant effect
on poisoning characteristics of metal hydride [38]. It was also reported
that, significant CO content in the impure gas mixture degrades the
absorption kinetics of the system in few purification cycles [42]. After
TCD analysis, the impure gas sample was sent for absorption to stage 1
reactor i.e. R1 (La0.9Ce0.1Ni5), maintaining absorption condition of 20
bar and 20 ◦ C. As depicted in Fig. 8b, the amount of hydrogen absorbed
in stage 1 reactor was 10.59 g in 262 s, attaining the peak bed tem­
perature of 60 ◦ C in 23 s and peak HTF temperature of nearly 22.5 ◦ C.
Despite of high supply pressure (20 bar) the rate of absorption was on
the slower side, because of the high impurity content in the absorption
gas sample. After completion of absorption half cycle, the reactor R1
was, further cooled down to 15 ◦ C, and prepared for flushing. During
flushing, the reactor was maintained at 15 ◦ C, and the discharge valve
was opened to flush the reactor to ambient conditions. The amount of
gas came out during flushing was recorded as 1.03 g, which lefts 9.56 g
hydrogen available for transfer within reactor R1. The flushed sample
was further analysed using online TCD.
As depicted in Fig. 8c, the gases detected in the flushed sample
constitute of 4.65% H2, 30.19% Ar, 31.88% N2 and 33.28% CH4.
However, due to significant poisoning characteristics of CO, it goes
through methanation to form methane and moisture, once it reacts with
hydrogen in presence of Ni of La0.9Ce0.1Ni5, and hence, it was not
detected in the flushed sample [55]. However, with lesser CO content
and high desorption temperature (above 80–85 ◦ C), the reactor re­
generates to absorb hydrogen in subsequent absorption cycle [42]. Apart
from these gases, CO2, must have been present in the desorption sample,
but it was not detected during TCD analysis because of molecular sieve
column used for the analysis. It can be observed that, during flushing,
impurities present in the void space of the reactor leaves the reactor with
very little amount of hydrogen i.e. 4.65%, which constitutes to 0.04 g of
total gas flushed. Hydrogen coming out during flushing is mostly the
unabsorbed hydrogen, which is present in the void space of the reactor
along with impurities. After successful flushing of the impurities from
R1, the reactor was prepared for desorption, wherein the reactor was
heated to 90 ◦ C.
Simultaneously, the stage 2 reactor (R2 filled with LaNi5) was pre­
pared for absorption. Before hydrogen transfer in the coupled reactor
system, R1 was maintained at 90 ◦ C and R2 was maintained at 20 ◦ C.
Once the respective bed and HTF temperatures stabilizes, the rector
valves were opened for facilitating hydrogen transfer from R1 to R2. The
amount of hydrogen transferred from R1 to R2 was 8.91 g in 330 s,
which constitute of 93.20% of total gas available for transfer. During this
process, the peak bed temperature of 49.5 ◦ C in 14 s and peak HTF
temperature of nearly 22.2 ◦ C was attained in the absorbing reactor R2
(Fig. 9a). However, because of endothermic desorption the bed and HTF
temperature of R1 dropped to 70.8 ◦ C and 87.6 ◦ C respectively. Further,
during gas transfer from R1 to R2, sample after stage 1 purification was
collected and analysed. As depicted in Fig. 9b, it was observed that, after
stage 1 purification, 91.54% pure hydrogen was obtained with impurity
of 8.46%. The impurities comprise of 2.54% Ar, 2.14% N2, 3.79% CH4
and trace of 0.01% CO. In the TCD results of the flushed gas sample

3.1.2. Hydrogen transfer from R1 to R3
Similarly, the experiments were performed to study the reaction ki­
netics of hydrogen transfer from R1 to R3 as coupled system, wherein
temperatures of the reaction bed were varied as per selected parameters
(Table 1). The rate of hydrogen transfer was directly proportional to the
difference between the absorbing and desorbing bed temperature. As
depicted in Fig. 6a, the rate and amount of hydrogen absorbed was less
for 70 ◦ C desorption temperature (R1) and 30 ◦ C absorption temperature
(R3), and it was maximum for 90 ◦ C desorption temperature (R1) and
20 ◦ C absorption temperature (R3). The amount of hydrogen transfer
varied from 11.948 g in 360 s to 13.37 g in 280 s, which was in the range
of 82.4–92.2% of the total hydrogen available for transfer. As shown in
Table 3, for the various operating temperatures, the reaction kinetics got
varied, which was mainly because of the difference in the equilibrium
bed pressure of the absorbing and desorption bed at different tempera­
ture. The endothermic/exothermic reactions can be clearly observed in
Fig. 6b and Fig. 6c, wherein the temperature variation plot for the
coupled reactor system is presented. Due to significant pressure differ­
ence for lower (20 ◦ C) absorbing and higher (90 ◦ C) desorbing MH bed
(Fig. 6b), the bed temperature variation has showed highest rise and
drop for the respective absorbing and desorbing beds.
3.1.3. Hydrogen transfer from R2 to R3
Similarly, hydrogen transfer between R2 and R3 was also studied.
For 70 ◦ C desorption temperature (R2) and 30 ◦ C absorption tempera­
ture (R2), the amount of hydrogen transfer was 11.72 g, which was
80.8% of the total hydrogen available for transfer. Hydrogen transfer
from R2 to R3 increased significantly with increase in the temperature of
desorbing reactor and decrease in the temperature of absorbing reactor
(Fig. 7a). For 90 ◦ C desorption temperature (R2) and 20 ◦ C absorption
temperature (R3), the amount of hydrogen transfer was 13.33 g, which
constitutes of nearly 92% of the total hydrogen available for transfer.
With increase in temperature difference between the absorbing and
desorbing reactors, the pressure gradient between the reactors also in­
creases. This leads to faster rate of hydrogen transfer between the re­
actors. Moreover, for lower absorption temperature, the equilibrium bed
pressure of the alloy is lower, this increases the rate of absorption for the
absorbing reactor. The details of the hydrogen transfer between the
coupled reactor R1 & R3 is depicted in Table 4. The temperature vari­
ation of the MHHPS and HTF during coupled reactor study is depicted in
Fig. 7b and Fig. 7c. The endothermic nature of the desorbing bed and the
exothermic reaction in the absorbing bed can be clearly observed. For all
the three cases, it was observed that for 90 ◦ C desorption temperature
and 20 ◦ C absorption temperature, the amount of hydrogen absorbed
was maximum. This is because of higher pressure difference between the
equilibrium pressure of desorbing reactor at 90 ◦ C and absorbing reactor
at 20 ◦ C. Therefore, it is suggested to maintain maximum temperature
difference between the absorbing and the desorbing reactor.

4. Conclusions
The present work reports the hydrogen storage and separation
characteristics of metal hydride based multistage hydrogen purification
system. The discussed system used different compositions of AB5 inter­
metallic alloys to separate hydrogen from an impure gas sample with
range of impurity in single or multistage. The multi-reactor system
showed hydrogen storage capacity of 1.3–1.4 wt%. Due to alloy tailoring
characteristics of the MH alloys, the equilibrium bed pressure can be
varied at room temperature, which is in the order La0.9Ce0.1Ni5 < LaNi5
15

A. Kumar and P. Muthukumar

Applied Energy 363 (2024) 123076

stage (50%–90%) impurity. The reversibility of the system can be in
the range of 90–99%, however, with further increase in the desorp­
tion temperature and decrease in the flushing temperature, the
hydrogen losses during different stages of purification can be
minimised.
• Regeneration of MH alloys are possible after alloy poisoning,
wherein reactivation of alloys can be performed by evacuating MH
reactor to 10− 2 mbar at 90–95 ◦ C, followed by absorption at 20–30
bar and 20–30 ◦ C.
• The absorption, flushing and desorption samples were collected for
TCD analysis using helium as carrier gas. The TCD analysis was found
to be effective method for detecting gas samples, however, because
of maximum impurities flushed during stage 1 purification, the gas
volume obtained after flushing of stage 2 and stage 3 reactors was not
enough to perform TCD analysis.
• The MH based hydrogen purification technology can be cost effec­
tive, as it can be operated using low grade thermal input, and with
minimal electrical input which can be drawn from solar. The core of
the system, i.e. the reactors, has no moving parts within it. This
minimises the maintenance cost of the system as there is no wear and
tear.
