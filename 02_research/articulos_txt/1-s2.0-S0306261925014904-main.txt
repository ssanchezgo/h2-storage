• A PEMFC–MH hydrogen supply system designed for hydrogen-powered bicycles (H-bike).

A compact hydrogen supply system for thermally integrating metal hydride (MH) tanks with a proton exchange
membrane fuel cell (PEMFC) for a hydrogen-powered electric-assist bicycle (H-bike) is proposed. The system
recovers the exhaust heat generated by the PEMFC to sustain hydrogen desorption and improve the system’s
energy efficiency. The results demonstrate that the split-tank strategy decreases thermal and pressure gradients
and enhances heat transfer and hydrogen release. The honeycomb tank configuration further improves hydrogen
desorption by promoting uniform airflow distribution around each tank, thereby improving exhaust heat utili­
zation from the PEMFC. It employs a layer-adjustable configuration, facilitating the flexible adaptation of MH
cartridge quantities to meet hydrogen demand and prevailing road conditions in urban areas. Under a PEMFC
power output of 215 W, the system maintains a stable hydrogen flow rate for over 30 min, with a heat recovery
efficiency of 22.62 %. Furthermore, increasing the number of MH cartridge layers significantly improves the
thermal utilization of the system, achieving a utilization efficiency of 39.90 % with two layers. These findings
confirm the feasibility and scalability of the proposed system for H-bike, highlighting its potential as a decen­
tralized hydrogen supply solution for lightweight mobility and urban transportation applications.

PEMFCs are crucial in hydrogen energy due to their high efficiency,
low operating temperatures, compactness, and rapid startup, making
them ideal for transport and portable applications [14–16]. Despite their
electrochemical benefits, PEMFCs produce substantial heat, emitting
about 40 % to 60 % of the hydrogen energy as exhaust heat [17–19].
Ineffective heat management can cause local overheating, membrane
dehydration, catalyst degradation, and reduce system lifespan. Not
using dissipated heat also lowers energy efficiency [20–22]. In con­
ventional designs, thermal energy is usually removed by air or liquid and
discharged into the surrounding environment, representing a lost op­
portunity for energy recovery [23,24]. Various methods have been
proposed for heat recovery; however, many are limited by their reliance
on complex or large system setups [25–27]. Therefore, creating passive,
compact, and integrated heat recovery solutions is essential for smallscale PEMFCs.
MH alloys represent another key technology and have attracted
substantial attention as a promising medium for hydrogen storage,
particularly in small-scale and portable applications [28,29]. Their main
advantages are high hydrogen density, safe low-pressure operation, and
good reversibility over cycles [30,31]. However, their inherent ther­
mophysical limitations greatly limit practical implementation. One of
the key challenges is the endothermic hydrogen desorption, where
insufficient external heat causes the MH bed temperature to drop,
lowering equilibrium pressure and hydrogen release rate [32,33]. In the
absence of external heating, their desorption rates may be insufficient to
meet the real-time hydrogen demand of PEMFCs, particularly under
dynamic or peak operating conditions [34,35]. Another key challenge is
the inherently low thermal conductivity of most MH materials [36,37].
This poor thermal conductivity results in significant internal tempera­
ture gradients during hydrogen desorption. Internal temperature gra­
dients create spatial variations in local equilibrium pressure during
desorption, which reduces the thermodynamic driving force [38]. As a
result, the core region of the MH bed cools much faster than the outer
shell, leading to uneven thermal distribution, nonuniform hydrogen
desorption, and ultimately, reduced hydrogen utilization efficiency
[39,40]. A nonuniform pressure distribution within MH tanks also poses
a key challenge [41]. The geometry of the MH tank contributes to
pressure non-uniformity. As hydrogen diffuses from the MH bed toward
the outlet, a pressure gradient is established due to the rapid discharge of
hydrogen at the outlet. Such pressure disparity negatively impacts
desorption efficiency [42,43]. This problem is further exacerbated in
elongated or large tanks, where the increased diffusion resistance im­
pedes hydrogen movement, resulting in pressure distribution and
reduced hydrogen desorption [44,45]. Addressing these combined
thermal and pressure limitations is crucial for advancing MH-based
hydrogen storage systems.
Numerous strategies have been proposed to improve thermal man­
agement, including adding internal fins with liquid cooling, designing
multi-tubular porous MH reactors with optimized thermophysical
properties, using activated‑carbon reactors combined with PCM and
metal fins, and developing hybrid PCM-metal foam composites to
enhance heat transfer [46–49]. However, these methods involve com­
plex reactor designs or complicated fabrication processes, which limit
their use in compact and mobile systems. In contrast, combining PEMFCgenerated exhaust heat with hydrogen desorption in MH tanks offers a
simpler and more practical way to improve overall system efficiency,
especially for mobile applications. Progress has been made in several
studies by utilizing thermal management techniques, resulting in
improved heat transfer and energy efficiency in hydrogen storage sys­
tems. For direct heat exchange, Liu et al. proposed a heat-coupled
PEMFC stack integrated with embedded MH containers, facilitating
direct thermal transfer. While improving heat utilization during

2. Methodology
2.1. Split-tank strategy for the MH tank
In natural and forced convection, as well as turbulent flow, the
exposed surface area significantly influences heat transfer efficiency
between the tank and its surrounding environment. An increased surface
area enhances the effectiveness of convective heat exchange, which is
particularly crucial for facilitating the endothermic hydrogen desorption
process in MH tanks [55]. Fig. 1 illustrates the geometry of the MH tanks
and the placement of their internal thermocouples. A traditional single
large MH tank was subdivided into six smaller tanks, maintaining an
equivalent total volume to enhance thermal exchange during hydrogen
desorption. This modular redesign involves an approximately 3.5-fold
increase in the total surface area compared to the traditional large
tank. The depicted dimensions represent the inner diameters, as the
thermal conductivity of the aluminum shell significantly exceeds that of
the MH alloy, rendering the internal structure a more critical factor for
thermal conductivity. In addition to the increased surface area, the
smaller tanks offer several advantages in terms of heat transfer charac­
teristics. Firstly, the reduced diameter and height of each tank consid­
erably shorten the heat conduction pathway from the tank wall to its
center, promoting quicker heat transfer and a more uniform temperature
distribution throughout the MH tank, along with an expected
improvement in the desorption capacity. Secondly, the compact geom­
etry of the small tanks facilitates a more uniform pressure distribution
and hydrogen flow, preventing the uneven utilization frequently

Thirdly, while a conventional cylindrical tank mainly exposes its lateral
surface to the surrounding air, the spatial design of the smaller tanks
allows nearly all sides to face the external environment. This greatly
increases the pathways for convective heat transfer, enhancing the
system’s dynamic responsiveness to environmental changes. These
thermal enhancements are expected to improve the temperature sta­

anticipated to facilitate the integration of exhaust heat from the PEMFC.
All MH tanks were filled with LaNi5, a substance widely employed in
small-scale mobile applications due to its high volumetric hydrogen
density and its capacity for efficient hydrogen absorption and desorption
within a near-ambient temperature range [28,56]. Thermocouples were
strategically incorporated at specific locations within the tanks to assess
their internal thermal characteristics. Further, a comprehensive tem­
perature mapping strategy was implemented in the larger tank to cap­
ture the axial and radial temperature distributions. Three sensors (A1,
B1, and C1) were installed along the longitudinal axis at distances of 15,
45, and 75 mm from the hydrogen outlet, respectively. Furthermore,
two sensors (B2 and B3) were embedded along the radial axis at depths
of 11 and 1 mm, respectively, from the inner tank wall. This configu­
ration facilitated the monitoring of the internal thermal conditions along
both axes. In the small tank configuration, a central thermocouple (D1)
was embedded at the geometric center to monitor the core temperature
during hydrogen desorption.

Therefore, designing a heat utilization strategy to both improve the heat
recovery rate of the PEMFC exhaust and ensure its cooling performance
is crucial.
This goal was achieved by adopting a honeycomb configuration; as
shown in Fig. 2, four small MH tanks, each with an outer diameter of 35
mm, form a layer and are arranged symmetrically in a cylindrical duct
with a diameter of 100 mm. A uniform gap of 4.5 mm is maintained
between each pair of adjacent tanks as well as between each tank and
the duct wall. This symmetrical and compact arrangement ensures an
even distribution of airflow around all tanks. Notably, the total surfaceto-volume ratio of the small tanks is significantly higher than that of a
single large tank with the same total capacity. The honeycomb structure
enhances interfacial heat transfer, stimulating local airflow turbulence
and further improving heat transfer. This configuration enables the MH
tanks to absorb heat from the PEMFC exhaust without leading to heat
accumulation, a scenario that could otherwise affect the cooling
behavior of the PEMFC. Two ducts were installed in parallel, each
capable of accommodating up to five layers of MH tanks (with four tanks
per layer), yielding 40 tanks in the current configuration. The number of
layers can be flexibly adjusted based on hydrogen demand and available
thermal input, providing adaptability for different operating scenarios.
This approach is designed to facilitate stable hydrogen desorption and
compact system integration, which are advantageous characteristics for
lightweight mobility applications, including H-bikes.

2.2. Honeycomb tank configuration and PEMFC exhaust integration

A PEMFC stack typically operates at a temperature exceeding that of
the ambient environment, releasing thermal energy to the surroundings
from its external surfaces via both convective and radiative mechanisms.
A considerable proportion of the generated heat is employed for the
internal phase change process associated with water evaporation, which
predominantly occurs within the membrane and catalyst layers of the
PEMFC [63,64]. The residual heat, unabsorbed by the internal compo­
nents, is expelled through forced airflow as exhaust, consequently aiding
in regulating and maintaining the stack temperature within its optimal
operational limits [65]. This recoverable exhaust heat can be quantita­
tively assessed using the formulas in Eqs. 1 and 2 [56]. The symbols,
definitions, and units used in the equations are summarized in Table 1.

The PEMFC in the system is rated at 400 W, serving as the primary
power source for the H-bike and representing the leading thermal energy
provider for hydrogen desorption. A power of 400 W is sufficient to meet
the propulsion needs of the H-bike on various terrains while supporting
the integration of auxiliary components, such as cooling fans [57]. The
PEMFC uses an air-cooling mechanism with dual fans to continuously
discharge exhaust gas that carries heat energy. A semi-enclosed ducted
heat recovery system was used to ensure that the exhaust gas is directed
through the duct containing the MH tanks, thereby maximizing the
utilization of the PEMFC exhaust heat. Maintaining the stable operation
of the PEMFC is crucial. Excessively high temperatures within the FC
stack may have negative impacts, including reduced electrochemical
efficiency; unstable water management; and the accelerated degrada­
tion of key materials, such as membranes and catalysts [58–60]. High
temperatures can also disrupt the internal pressure balance and increase
ohmic losses, reducing the energy conversion efficiency [61,62].

2.4. Experimental apparatus and experimental procedures
The experimental platform utilized in this study is schematically
depicted in Fig. 3. It comprises a PEMFC power module, modular MH
tanks, a power load controller, a hydrogen flow regulation unit, and a
temperature and pressure monitoring system. The PEMFC is the elec­
trical power supply and the primary thermal energy source for hydrogen
desorption. This PEMFC is air-cooled via dual integrated fans, which
provide oxygen for the electrochemical reaction. MH tanks are arranged
in a honeycomb-style configuration and aligned within a semi-enclosed
exhaust duct to ensure optimal heat recovery. Two such ducts were
installed in parallel, each supporting up to five layers of MH cartridges.
A pressure-reducing valve was installed in the hydrogen supply line
upstream of the PEMFC inlet to ensure that hydrogen is delivered at the
appropriate pressure. Flow meters and pressure gauges were positioned
between the tanks and the fuel cell to continuously monitor the
hydrogen flow rate and internal pressure. Furthermore, thermocouples
were embedded near the center of each MH tank and at the cartridge
joints to capture real-time temperature data. All signals from thermo­
couples, pressure transducers, and flow sensors were collected through a
data acquisition system interfaced with LabVIEW software for real-time
monitoring and recording.
A decoupled hydrogen flow configuration was employed to evaluate
the hydrogen desorption behavior of the MH tanks under various con­
ditions. Before testing, the MH tanks were fully charged with hydrogen
at 0.3 MPa. The PEMFC received hydrogen from a high-pressure
hydrogen cylinder during the desorption experiments, while the MH
tanks were positioned in the exhaust flow duct. The hydrogen released
from the MH tanks was discharged into the atmosphere through a

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Hydrogen desorption behavior and superiority of the split-tank
strategy
Hydrogen desorption experiments were conducted on both the large
and small tank configurations to assess the effectiveness of the split-tank
strategy. The tests were executed under natural convection at room
temperature, with an initial internal hydrogen pressure of 0.3 MPa.
Hydrogen was released into the atmosphere at maximum flow rates of
1000 and 1125 sccm for the large and small tanks, respectively. The
temperature was monitored in real time using thermocouples placed at
predefined positions within the tanks, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Further­
more, natural convection and room temperature conditions were chosen
to eliminate uncertainties arising from forced or turbulent airflow in­
teractions with various tank geometries. This approach ensured that
performance differences were due to the tanks’ geometric and thermal
features, providing a reliable basis for predicting behavior in complex
conditions like forced convection or turbulent flow.

reduced thermal and hydrogen conduction paths, along with the
increased surface area, enable the smaller tanks to improve the system’s

insights can be gained by considering the total volume of hydrogen
released, as the small tank achieves a slightly better cumulative output.

tank (1125 sccm) using Eq. 8. This shows the greater hydrogen release
capacity of the small tank despite a higher thermal load, confirming the
efficiency of the modular design in managing transient heat transfer.
During the decline stage, the small tank exhibits a slower decrease in
flow rate, further indicating its superior heat exchange under natural
convection. Additionally, its narrow interstitial spaces and staggered
arrangement are expected to improve convective performance under
forced or turbulent conditions by causing localized disturbances.
In summary, conventional large MH tanks suffer from two significant
limitations: (i) low thermal conductivity causes severe overcooling at
the core and local cessation of desorption, and (ii) gas transport resis­
tance and uneven pressure distribution intensify temperature gradients,
reducing overall hydrogen release. In contrast, smaller modular tanks
alleviate these issues, and their performance is expected to be further
enhanced by elevated temperatures and intensified airflow, resulting in
superior desorption compared to large-tank designs.

3.2. Impact of exhaust heat recovery on PEMFC performance
The PEMFC was actively cooled using integrated fans, making its
exhaust flow crucial for dissipating excess heat from the stacks. In terms
of exhaust heat recovery, implementing a duct system to direct exhaust
toward the MH tanks introduces potential concerns regarding thermal
accumulation around the stacks, which may reduce the cooling effi­
ciency [67]. Consequently, it is imperative to note that the proposed
design does not interfere with the normal operations of the PEMFC.
Current-voltage (I-V) measurements were conducted under two designs
to evaluate the PEMFC performance: standard operation, and operation
via the honeycomb configuration exhaust heat recovery system. As
illustrated in Fig. 5(a), the I-V characteristics and corresponding power
output curves under both conditions display almost identical behaviors
over the current range. This consistency confirms that the honeycomb
configuration system does not jeopardize the electrical stability or
output performance of the PEMFC. The maximum output exceeds 300
W, meeting the power requirements of the H-bike.
In addition to evaluating the electrical performance, the thermal
characteristics of the exhaust at various power levels were assessed. As
depicted in Fig. 5(b), exhaust gas temperatures were recorded at three
representative PEMFC outputs—0, 54, and 215 W—respectively corre­
sponding to human-powered operation, flat-road cruising, and climbing
a 30◦ incline on an electric-assist bicycle [57]. The measurements were
used to assess the potential of exhaust heat as a passive heat source for
warming the MH tanks. The exhaust temperature increases with the
output power, reaching approximately 35 ◦ C at an output of 215 W. This
temperature range creates a favorable thermal environment conducive
to hydrogen desorption. Even under partial loads, the exhaust temper­
ature consistently exceeds ambient levels, indicating that sound thermal
energy can be recovered across a broad spectrum of operating
conditions.
3.3. Effect of PEMFC exhaust heat on hydrogen desorption
The effect of PEMFC exhaust heat on hydrogen desorption was
examined under four conditions: natural convection and PEMFC power
outputs of 0 W, 54 W, and 215 W. In the natural convection case, the MH
tank was exposed to still ambient air at room temperature without fanassisted airflow, so heat removal depended solely on natural circulation.
By contrast, the 0 W case also lacked PEMFC heat input but included fan
operation, creating forced convection around the tank. All tests used a
single-layer tank with an initial hydrogen flow rate of 1000 sccm.

the pressure-composition-temperature (PCT) characteristics of the MH,
where a decrease in temperature sharply lowers the equilibrium pres­
sure and desorption rate [68]. Internal temperature gradients exacer­
bate this effect, as areas that cool below the desorption threshold cease
releasing hydrogen, thereby reducing the overall desorption capacity.
Around 500 s under the 215 W condition, the temperature begins to
recover due to limited internal heat transport and the increasing
gradient between the surface and core. The low thermal conductivity of
the MH alloy delays the core response, while the rising surface tem­
perature enhances conduction inward. Over time, heat accumulates
until a steady state is reached, where heat transfer to the tank balances
the enthalpy absorption rate of 22.9 W, thereby stabilizing the
temperature.
Fig. 6(b) illustrates the corresponding pressure variations within the
MH tanks, with the internal pressure declining the fastest in the absence
of exhaust heat. As the PEMFC output increases, the rate of pressure
decline decreases, with the slowest decline observed at a power output
of 215 W. The internal tank pressure is primarily governed by the dy­
namic equilibrium between the hydrogen released from the MH alloy
and the hydrogen discharged from the tank outlet. Consequently, at a
power output of 215 W, the slower rate of pressure decrease suggests
that the system exhibits the highest hydrogen release rate from the MH
alloy among the tested conditions. This occurs because as the temper­
ature decreases, the equilibrium pressure also diminishes due to the PCT
characteristics, reducing the thermodynamic driving force for hydrogen
desorption. The continuous pressure decrease indicates that the rate of
hydrogen desorption from the MH alloy remains below the target
outflow rate of 1000 sccm. This imbalance ultimately results in a sharp
decline in the hydrogen flow rate under the conditions of natural con­
vection and power outputs of 0 and 54 W. In contrast, at a power output
of 215 W, the higher thermal input not only helps maintain a higher
average tank temperature but also increases the equilibrium pressure.
When the internal pressure reaches approximately 0.12 MPa, the rate of
hydrogen desorption from the MH alloy equals the hydrogen outflow
rate of 1000 sccm, leading to a dynamic equilibrium. These findings
confirm that 215 W of exhaust heat effectively supports stable pressure
dynamics and continuous hydrogen desorption, further validating the
superior thermal management capability of the system under this
condition.
Fig. 6(c) illustrates the impact of the exhaust heat on the hydrogen

flow rate. In the absence of exhaust heat, the flow rate experiences a
rapid decline, dropping below 1000 sccm within 300 s. At power outputs
of 0 and 54 W, the decline starts at approximately 350 and 450 s,
respectively. Conversely, the MH tank exhibits a stable flow rate of 1000
sccm for over 30 min at a power output of 215 W; the flow remains
stable until the experiment is manually stopped. Considering the above
analysis, the system is deemed to have reached a state of thermal
equilibrium at this stage, where the enthalpy required for hydrogen
desorption is effectively balanced by the heat absorbed from the external
environment. Thus, most of the hydrogen stored in the tank can be
efficiently released under this condition.
Table 2 presents a summary of the exhaust heat generated by the
PEMFC, along with the enthalpy absorbed by the MH, under various
conditions. The analysis is based on the period over which the hydrogen
release rate was maintained at 1000 sccm. Under the output conditions
of 0 and 54 W, the PEMFC exhausts 1.01 and 7.59 kJ of heat, respec­
tively. On the other hand, the enthalpy absorbed by the MH is dispro­
portionately high compared to the supplied heat, reaching 7.90 and
10.08 kJ, respectively. This indicates that the thermal energy provided
under these circumstances is inadequate to satisfy the continuous
enthalpy demand of the desorption reaction. In contrast, under a power
output of 215 W, the total thermal energy supplied is 182.24 kJ, 41.22
kJ of which is absorbed by the hydride material. As the system reaches a
state of thermal equilibrium, its heat utilization rate reaches 22.62 %. As
such an equilibrium state was not achieved under the other conditions,
the utilization rates could not be accurately determined in those cases;
however, the values are lower than 22.62 %.
In conclusion, the condition involving a power output of 215 W
surpasses all other conditions in maintaining elevated internal temper­
atures, a slower pressure decay rate, and a sustained hydrogen flow rate,
leading to enhanced thermal and desorption stability. It confirms that
effectively recovering PEMFC exhaust heat significantly enhances the
thermal stability of MH tanks, mitigates internal pressure decay, and
increases the period of stable hydrogen supply. The proposed integration
strategy promotes effective thermal coupling, eliminating the need for
external heating and enhancing the overall system performance and
energy efficiency.

19.95 %. These results confirm that with sufficient exhaust power,
increasing the number of MH layers significantly enhances heat utili­

hydrogen demand, ensuring the complete release of hydrogen stored
within the cartridges. Beyond this two-layer setup, the PEMFC stack
possesses a baseline weight of 5 kg and a volume of 6.1 L, with each
additional MH layer contributing approximately 1.36 kg and 1.26 L.
Augmenting the number of layers also increases the thermal distance
between the exhaust source and the outer cartridges, which may
diminish heat transfer efficiency and induce pressure drops. Neverthe­
less, as demonstrated by Eq. 8, allocating the thermal load across a
greater number of cartridges enables each layer to attain higher steadystate temperatures, thus enhancing overall heat utilization efficiency.
The superior performance of multilayer configurations is attributable to
the semi-independent operation of each cartridge and the increased heat
exchange surface area provided by the honeycomb structure, which
extends the exhaust-tank interaction period and optimizes heat
recovery.

heat recovery from FCs. The results show that conventional proton ex­

stationary applications. Solid oxide fuel cell-organic Rankine cycle

generator (PEMFC-TEG) systems recover less than 1 % of the energy due
to the low temperature gradients. In contrast, the proposed honeycomb
multilayer PEMFC-MH setup achieves a utilization efficiency of 39.90 %
under 215 W exhaust conditions, featuring a simple design optimized for
small-scale H-bike applications. This demonstrates the benefits of
modular multilayer MH integration for better waste heat utilization in
portable fuel cell systems.
4. Conclusion
This study presents a novel tank structure and honeycomb configu­
ration, offering an integrated hydrogen supply system that utilizes
exhaust heat from PEMFC to enhance desorption performance in MH
tanks. The key findings are summarized as follows:
1. The split-tank strategy shows that dividing a large MH tank into
smaller cartridges effectively reduces heat conduction and hydrogen
diffusion paths, enhances thermal uniformity, and relieves localized
overcooling. This greatly improves hydrogen release capacity by
addressing the thermal bottlenecks present in large tanks.
2. Effective heat recovery with a honeycomb tank setup. The honey­
comb tank passively captures exhaust heat without impacting the
PEMFC’s cooling efficiency or power output. At a power level of 215
W, the system reaches thermal equilibrium and maintains a steady
hydrogen flow rate of 1000 sccm for over 30 min. The heat recovery
efficiency of 22.62 % demonstrates the viability of direct thermal
coupling between the PEMFC and MH.
3. Using multilayer cartridges increases the effective heat exchange
area and distributes the thermal load, allowing each layer to reach
higher steady-state temperatures. With two layers, the system sus­
tains a hydrogen flow of 2000 sccm for 30 min at 215 W, achieving a
utilization efficiency of 39.90 %. This confirms both scalability and
improved thermal utilization efficiency compared to single-layer
designs.
Overall, these strategies tackle the main thermal challenges of MH
tanks—low thermal conductivity, uneven pressure distribution, and
limited heat recovery. By improving heat transfer, boosting desorption
stability, and increasing heat utilization efficiency, the proposed design
offers a strong path for integrating PEMFC exhaust heat into MH sys­
tems. This modular and scalable approach provides a practical solution

5. Future Work
Although thermal equilibrium was successfully achieved using an
output power of 215 W, allowing for the release of most of the stored
hydrogen, the internal tank pressure stabilized at approximately 0.12
MPa. Given the diverse pressure requirements of commercial PEMFC
systems, future research efforts will focus on maintaining elevated
minimum tank pressures by increasing the number of MH tank layers
beyond that of the existing configuration. These considerations further
emphasize the need for a five-layer design to ensure adequate hydrogen
release and compatibility with a broader range of PEMFC systems.
