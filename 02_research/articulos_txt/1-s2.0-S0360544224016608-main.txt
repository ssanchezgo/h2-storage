A B S T R A C T

Handling Editor: A. Olabi

A novel integrated system comprising plasma gasification, small modular reactor (SMR) power generation,
pressure swing adsorption (PSA) hydrogen production, and Cu–Cl cycle thermochemical hydrogen generation
has been proposed for combined electricity and hydrogen co-production. In this innovative configuration, the
high-temperature syngas generated by the plasma gasifier is first cooled by the steam from the SMR power plant.
It subsequently undergoes water gas shift and is purified using a PSA unit to yield hydrogen product. Meanwhile,
the steam generated by SMR is utilized entirely for power generation during daytime peak hours while during
night time off-peak hours, a portion of the steam is diverted to provide heat for the Cu–Cl cycle, thereby facil­
itating load leveling. A comprehensive assessment of the hybrid system has been conducted from both ther­
modynamic and economic perspectives. Under two operational modes the efficiency of medical waste-to-energy
efficiency reaches 63.75 % (daytime) and 63.58 % (night time), with exergy efficiencies of 45.31 % (daytime)
and 44.17 % (night time). Furthermore, the net present value (NPV) of the new design is 81,727.18 k$, dynamic
payback period (DPP) of 4.34 years and an internal rate of return (IRR) of 41 %. These results substantiate the
feasibility of the design from thermodynamic and economic standpoints.

modeling, and also evaluated the performance, which was shown to be
superior to conventional waste incineration technologies. Wang et al.
[14] introduce a solar-nuclear power system using a solar tower and a

gasification with ionic CO2 capture for hydrogen from medical waste,
examining factors affecting syngas and efficiency. Martins et al. [16]
evaluated three gasification methods for green hydrogen; supercritical
water gasification had the highest yield yet was economically unfeasi­

The current research landscape lacks a comprehensive energy pro­
duction system that is modular, flexibly deployable, and environmen­

utilization of hazardous waste while reducing reliance on fossil fuels.
This study adopts static planning and innovatively proposes an inte­
grated system, a novel fusion of plasma gasification, SMR, PSA for
hydrogen production, and the Cu–Cl cycle. SMR is ideal for optimal heat
integration with a plasma gasifier due to its low central steam temper­

modular nuclear power plant, which achieves a two-way value-added
energy. During peak periods, all the steam is used to generate electricity
to meet the demand of the grid, while during low periods, some of the
steam is diverted to supply the heat demand of the hydrogen production
process in the Cu–Cl cycle. The modular aspect of plasma gasifiers and

integration. The modular design simplifies maintenance, facilitates

In light of the escalating global concerns over environmental
degradation and the urgent call to address carbon emissions [1], two
critical issues emerge at the nexus: the dangerous implications of med­
ical waste on the environment and the imperative of transitioning to
cleaner energy sources [2]. The environmental hazards of medical waste
have become increasingly evident, undermining conventional waste
disposal methods and underscoring the need for innovative waste
management approaches. The improper handling and incineration of
medical waste have been correlated with the contamination of air,
water, and soil and the emission of hazardous compounds that sub­
stantially threaten to ecological balance. Concurrently, pursuing effec­
tive strategies to reduce carbon emissions advances the exploration of
cleaner energy alternatives in striving for a more sustainable energy
paradigm [3].
Consequently, the imperative to develop efficient, safe, and sus­
tainable waste treatment technologies stands as a critical concern within
the scientific and industrial realms. Plasma gasification technology has
captured significant interest recently and has been touted for its
distinctive capacity to manage hazardous solid waste [4]. As a process
characterized by high temperatures and intense energy input, plasma
gasification converts waste material into a plasma state, disintegrating
both organic and inorganic substances to their fundamental elements
[5]. This method is beneficial in diminishing the volume of hazardous
solid waste and decomposing harmful compounds, thus reducing the
risk of environmental pollution. Additionally, conventional waste
incineration power plants demonstrate relatively lower electrical effi­
ciency, with figures ranging from 14 % to 28 % [6]. Nevertheless,
achieving the high-efficiency profile of plasma gasification necessitates
significant investment and operational costs. These expenses could be
compensated for by the synergistic integration with SMR, which further
enhances the overall efficiency of the process.
In contrast to traditional nuclear reactors, SMRs feature simplified,
standardized designs and inherent safety characteristics [7]. Neverthe­
less, SMRs typically generate steam at substantially lower temperatures
than coal-fired plants resulting in comparatively lower thermoelectric
conversion efficiency [8]. Integrating SMRs with plasma gasification
improves energy efficiency through the utilization of heat from synthesis
gas, which raises the temperature of steam and feedwater in SMR power
plants. This higher operating temperature enables the turbines in SMR
power plants to generate more electricity.
Modular nuclear power units are primarily designed to provide a
consistent supply of base-load electricity, with an emphasis on opti­
mized design and operation tailored to meet steady power demands.
However, this inherent stability limits their capacity to rapidly modulate
power outputs in response to sharp fluctuations in electricity demand
[9]. The limited flexibility of modular nuclear units in the face of sig­
nificant demand swings could lead to grid instability. It is important to
note that the reduced load-following capacity of nuclear reactors is not
solely a result of thermal inertia; rather, it is intimately connected to the
reactor’s predetermined operational parameters. These parameters are
orchestrated for optimal performance under specific conditions, and
adjustments to reactor inlet and outlet conditions to alter these param­
eters can lead to performance inefficiencies. While power output can be
modulated by decreasing operational intensity, such adjustments typi­
cally do not translate to proportional reductions in production costs or
extend the fuel lifecycle. Therefore, from both economic and techno­
logical perspectives, modifying reactors to enhance load-following is not
generally advised [10]. If modular nuclear power primarily focuses on
stable base-load electricity generation, investments to improve
load-following capability may not provide equal returns. The afore­
mentioned statement elucidates the intrinsic complexities involved in
the integration of load-following capabilities into modular nuclear re­
actors. It underscores the imperative for comprehensive assessments
that span technical aptitude, safety protocols, and financial feasibility.

The raw syngas generated by the plasma gasifier exhibits a sub­
stantially elevated temperature, peaking at 1072 ◦ C. Before the syngas
can be utilized, it needs to be cooled down. Reducing syngas tempera­
ture to 400 ◦ C is imperative to accommodate the water-gas shift reac­
tion. Notably, the thermal energy of syngas is reclaimed to bolster the
overall efficiency of the system. Within the framework of the SMR power
plant, this thermal energy is channelized to preheat the primary steam
and feedwater, thus capitalizing on the heat generated.
Subsequent to cooling, the syngas is amalgamated with steam from
heat exchanger 2 (HX-2) and channeled into the two-stage water gas
shift (WGS) reactor, catalyzing its conversion into a hydrogen-rich gas.
Owing to the exothermic characteristics of the WGS reaction, HX-2 and
heat exchanger 1 (HX-1) have been positioned downstream of the hightemperature water gas shift (HT-WGS) reactor and the low-temperature
water gas shift (LT-WGS) reactor, respectively. This arrangement aims to
effectively capture and utilize the heat generated during the WGS re­
action [20], thereby heating water to engender steam requisite for the
process, and ensuring efficient energy management. Subsequently, in
order to protect the PSA unit and to improve plant efficiency, undesir­
able constituents within the modified syngas, including COS, NH3, H2S,
and moisture, are excised in the cleaning unit. The resultant purified
syngas is then conveyed to the tertiary PSA unit to produce the hydrogen
and carbon dioxide via pressure swing adsorption.
The novel hybrid system features bifurcated operational modes,
tailored to the diurnal fluctuations in electricity demand: the daytime
operation mode during the peak electricity consumption period and the
nighttime operation mode during the low electricity consumption
period. The crux of differentiation between these two operational states
resides within the operational dynamics of the SMR power plant
segment.

3. Methodology
3.1. Model development
The proposed novel hybrid system is co-simulated by Aspen plus and
EBSILON Professional as sketched in Fig. 2. The plasma gasification
subsystem, the PSA hydrogen production subsystem, and the Cu–Cl
cycle subsystem were modeled by Aspen plus [21]. EBSILON Profes­
sional was employed to model the SMR power plant [22].
3.1.1. Plasma gasification subsystem
Plasma gasification offers a promising method for medical waste
disposal, employing high temperatures for pyrolysis and electron
detachment to create a plasma state. The process, simulated by the
DECOMP chemical stoichiometry and high-temperature reactor (HTR)
modules, breaks down waste into basic elements such as C, H, O, N, S,
H2O, and ash. These elements are quantified using a state calculator in
Fortran. The HTR module, operating at roughly 2500 ◦ C, provides the
necessary thermal energy, driving reactions like dissociation, oxidation,
and reduction within the plasma. This leads to the production of gaseous
byproducts such as hydrogen, carbon monoxide, methane, forming
syngas, with key reactions detailed in Table A1 of the appendix.
3.1.2. PSA subsystem
The produced syngas is fed to the WGS reactor for further reactions,
containing a catalyst based on cobalt-molybdenum (CO/MO). The re­
actions that occur within the WGS reactors are shown in Equation (A13)
and Equation (A14) in the Appendix.
The H2-rich gas produced by the reaction is cooled to 35 ◦ C and
compressed to 30 atm using a multi-stage compressor. The H2-rich gas is
then sequentially processed through PAS-1, PSA-2, and PSA-3 units to
separate hydrogen gas from carbon dioxide, with the carbon dioxide
being sequestered. The operating parameters for the PSA subsystem are
detailed in Table 2.
3.1.3. Cu–Cl cycle subsystem
The hydrogen production methodology employed in this study is the
three-step Cu–Cl cycle, comprising distinct hydrolysis, decomposition,
and electrolysis reactions. Each reaction stage is conducted within
dedicated chemical stoichiometric reactors under specific conditions.
The reactions and their corresponding specified conditions are outlined
as follows. The operating parameters of the Cu–Cl cycle are shown in
Table 3.
The chemical equation (A15) in Appendix presented represents the
hydrolysis reaction, which is simulated by the “Hydrolysis” chemical
stoichiometric reactor module. The hydrolysis reaction is conducted
within an essential thermal window of 350–400 ◦ C, and it proceeds
under ambient pressure conditions. The required thermal energy for this
reaction is provisioned by steam generated from the SMR power plant
[23].
The decomposition reaction, characterized by the chemical equation
(A16) in Appendix, is simulated through employment of the chemical
stoichiometric reactor module termed “Decomposition”. This reaction
occurs typically within the temperature range of 500–540 ◦ C and
operates under ambient pressure. The reactant for the decomposition
reaction, Cu2OCl2 generated from the hydrolysis reaction, is introduced
into the reactor for the designated reaction sequence.
Following the cooling of the mixture derived from the hydrolysis
reaction, comprising HCl and H2O, as well as the Cu–Cl produced from
the decomposition reaction, the resulting mixture is introduced into the
chemical stoichiometric reactor named “Electrolysis” to initiate the
electrochemical reaction. This reaction yields H2 as the ultimate prod­
uct. The electrolysis reaction occurs under standard temperature con­
ditions and at a pressure of 25 bar. The equations for the above three
reactions are shown in Equations A15-16 in the Appendix.

(a) Operating mode of the system at daytime
During the daytime hours of peak electricity consumption, Valve-1 is
completely shut down and all of the steam generated from the SMR is
used to generate electricity to power the grid.
(b) Operating mode of the system at night
During nocturnal phases characterized by diminished electricity
demand, Valve-1 is actuated, while Valve-2 is adjusted to a partially
closed state. A proportion of steam from the SMR unit is diverted to
supply thermal energy for the hydrolytic and thermal decomposition
reactions within the Cu–Cl cycle. This process effectuates a reduction in
electric output from the SMR facility while facilitating peak load
adaptation. After providing heat to the Cu–Cl cycle, the steam, retaining
a temperature of 287 ◦ C, is subsequently routed back to the steam tur­
bine for power generation. The Cu–Cl cycle is sustained by the intro­
duction of water, which undergoes hydrolysis, thermal decomposition,
and electrolysis, mediated by Cu and Cl ions, to bifurcate into hydrogen
and oxygen. This scheme effectively transmutes a fraction of the elec­
trical output from the SMR installation into hydrogen, thus enabling a
peak load-shifting capability within modular nuclear systems.
With the above new hybrid system, modular nuclear power can
achieve efficient peak shifting and, at the same time, the conversion of
hazardous medical waste to clean and safe energy.

The IRR is a financial metric assessing investment profitability,
representing the annualized compound rate of return for projects with
varying cash flows. Based on the present value of the cash flows and the
real rate of return target, the discount rate is continuously adjusted until
a discount rate is found which makes the sum of the present values of the
cash flows equal to zero. When the sum of the present values of the cash
flows is equal to zero, this discount rate is the desired internal rate of
return, IRR. IRR helps determine project worthiness; if it exceeds the
investor’s required rate of return, the project is considered attractive as
it yields returns above expectations [32].

4.3. Exergy analysis
The transformation of energy within a system is not solely confined
to its quantitative aspect; rather, it hinges on the quality and availability
of energy. In this context, exergy is a pivotal metric for assessing
available energy. It unveils losses incurred during energy conversion
and presents opportunities for system optimization. Exergy analyses
were conducted on the SMR power plant and the proposed hybrid sys­
tem, as depicted in Table 12. The reference unit’s exergy input for the
SMR power plant was constant, denoted as 100 %. Due to the integration
of the proposed system, an additional exergy input of 12.46 MW from
medical waste was introduced. The tabulated data reveals that under
daytime operational mode, the overall exergy losses for the system
amount to 17.82 MW, while under nighttime operational mode, these
losses escalate to 18.23 MW. The system-wide exergy efficiency for the
two operational modes is 45.31 % (daytime) and 44.17 % (nighttime).
The diminishment in exergy efficiency from daytime to nighttime is
minimal, ensuring efficient peak-shaving capabilities for the system.
Notably, the overall exergy efficiency is enhanced compared to the
reference SMR power plant’s exergy efficiency of 34.03 %. As illustrated
in Fig. 6, within the hybrid system, the SMR power plant exhibits the
highest irreversibility, followed by the PSA subsystem, accounting for
17.9 % of the total exergy losses. Discrepancies in temperature between

5. Conclusions
To enhance the peak shaving capability of the SMR and to environ­
mentally manage hazardous solid waste, this study has undertaken the
reconfigurable design of a hybrid system building upon the foundation
of the SMR power plant. This conceptual hybrid configuration encom­
passes the SMR power plant, a plasma gasification subsystem, a PSA
subsystem, and a Cu–Cl cycle subsystem. Comprehensive technical and
economic performance analyses of the hybrid system were carried out.
Several key findings emerge from this investigation.
