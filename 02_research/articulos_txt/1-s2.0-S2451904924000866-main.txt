The efficient operation of a metal hydride based hydrogen storage system primarily depends on the configuration
of the storage reactor. Both the total weight and effective performance are crucial for large capacity hydrogen
storage systems. Therefore, the present work is centered around the development of a lightweight, compact, and
efficient MH reactor capable of accommodating a large hydrogen inventory. In this perspective, a disc finned
multi tube reactor (DFMTR) design is proposed, and its behaviour is numerically analyzed. Five reactor con­
figurations are studied using 3D COMSOL modeling to visualize the impact of various fin structures during the
absorption and desorption half cycles. The DFMTR model shows remarkably higher performance by saving 38%
and 31% time to store and release hydrogen compared to the baseline reactor. Further, the design methodology is
discussed, and the geometrical and operational parameters are optimized to achieve the excellent performance of
the proposed reactor. Furthermore, the portable design approach for scaling up the capacity of the storage system
is discussed and recommended. Moreover, the proposed reactor design is compared with recent studies of the
literature, and found that this reactor saved 22.1%, 50%, 52.1%, and 8.7% of time to store 90% hydrogen
compared with tapered finned multi tube reactor, tube bundle reactor, radial finned reactor, and pin fin reactor,
respectively. The authors believe that the proposed large capacity reactor has the potential to deliver outstanding
performance, and this research will be instrumental in the construction of compact, effective metal hydride
reactors with significant hydrogen storage capacities.

Introduction
It is imperative to expand the utilization of renewable resources on a
larger scale by considering future energy requirements and the
increasing carbon emissions in the world [1]. To accomplish the ob­
jectives of Paris Agreement, world emissions must be reduced by 30% by
2030, as reported by the Bloomberg Energy Outlook 2021 [2]. In this
regard, finding an energy carrier that solves the intermittent issue of
renewables and satisfies future energy needs without any negative ef­
fects on the environment is crucial [3]. Hydrogen is gaining popularity
as a source in wide applications because it is a cleaner, abundant, flex­
ible, and efficient energy carrier. The hydrogen energy economy in­
cludes hydrogen generation, storage, transportation, and its end use.
Further, hydrogen offers an appealing choice since, whether it’s burned
for heat generation or combined with air in fuel cells to generate elec­
tricity, the only resulting by product is water. Nowadays, hydrogen can
be generated using commercial electrolyzers, which can provide

hydrogen up to a pressure of 30 bar [4]. However, the development of
safer, more efficient, and more affordable hydrogen storage technology
is crucial. The traditional methods of storing green hydrogen, such as
cryogenic liquid and compressed gas in high pressure vessels, consume
up to 30% of the stored hydrogen energy content [5]. In recent years,
sorption hydrogen storage using metal hydrides has gained attention
due to its higher energy density (~140 MJ.kg− 1) [6]. In general, the MH
systems are thermally driven, in which exothermic and endothermic
chemical reactions occur during the absorption and desorption half cy­
cles. The rate of heat transfer should be high enough to permit faster and
continuous hydrogen gas storage and discharge. The metal hydrides
(MHs) have poor thermal conductivity (~1 W.m− 1K− 1), they require an
effective heat transfer system [7–9]. Hence, for the efficient perfor­
mance of a metal hydride system, the heat transfer rate is a crucial
factor. Metal hydride hydrogen storage systems (MHHSS) can be inte­
grated with electrolyzers to store the produced hydrogen under atmo­
spheric conditions. Moreover, this thermally driven system requires only
waste heat (in the range of 40–100 ◦ C) for hydrogen retrieval.

Over the years, various techniques, including innovative reactor
geometries and the incorporation of heat enhancive fillers, have been
reported in the literature to boost heat transfer within MH reactors.
These techniques include the incorporation of different heat transfer
tubes and coils [10–21], diverse extended surfaces [22–29], expanded
natural graphite (ENG) compacts [30–32], and metallic fillers [33–35]
within the MH reactor. Gkanas et al. [21] conducted a numerical study
on the optimization of the rectangular type reactor for hydrogen storage
applications. They studied the impact of varied bed thickness and tube
positions and found the optimized thickness between two cooling or
heating surfaces as 10.4 mm under a heat transfer coefficient of 2000 W.
m− 2K− 1. Raju et al. [12] carried out the experimental study on 4 kg of
LaNi4.7Al0.3 powder inside a 55 cooling tube reactor. They found that
46.2 g of hydrogen was absorbed by the alloy in 5 min at inlet pressure
and heat transfer fluid (HTF) temperature of 20 bar and 293 K, respec­
tively. Anbarasu et al. [36] carried out an experimental study on
embedded cooling tube (ECT) reactors containing 2.75 kg of
LmNi4.91Sn0.15 alloy. They compared two reactors (36 ECT reactor and
60 ECT reactor) and found that increasing the cooling tubes from 36 to
60 reduced the time taken by the alloy to reach 1.04 wt.% from 480 to
360 s under 25 bar supply pressure. Chung et al. [37] conducted an
experimental study on reactors with or without heat pipes. They found
the absorption time was dropped from 2100 s to 900 s by using a heat
pipe as compared to not using a heat pipe under a gas pressure of 10 bar
and HTF temperature of 293 K. Mellouli et al. [38] investigated the
single helical tube reactor that contained 1 kg of LaNi5 powder and
found that the reactor took 25 min to reach 90% of its hydrogen capacity
at 10 bar supply pressure. Also, the two helical tubes took 8.33 min, and
one helical tube with fins also took the same time (8.33 min) to reach
90% saturation under similar operating conditions. Muthukumar et al.
[15] formulated a two dimensional model filled with MmNi4.6Al0.4 and
found that increasing the cooling tubes from 12 to 20 reduces the time to
reach 90% saturation from 296 s to 155 s, respectively, under a gas
pressure of 20 bar and an HTF temperature of 30 ◦ C.
Numerous studies in the literature have suggested that a combina­
tion of tubes and fins can significantly enhance the effectiveness of the
MH reactor. Parida and Muthukumar [23] numerically investigated the
impact of dissimilar fin types, namely longitudinal, transverse, and
spiral, on reactor performance. They concluded that longitudinal and
transverse fins indicated slightly better performance during absorption
and desorption processes, respectively. Further, the designed reactor
was able to deliver hydrogen at 2.27 lpm till 2000 s at a discharge
pressure of 10 bar. Askri et al. [24] investigated the different heat
transfer systems. The reactor without any thermal management, took
2700 s to reach 90% of its maximum capacity under the 8 bar gas

pressure and HTF temperature of 293 K. Also, the absorption time was
reduced to 2080 s, 980 s, and 460 s by adding the exterior fins, cooling
tube, and cooling tube with internal fins, respectively. Singh et al. [25]
conducted an experimental study on the MH reactor containing a U
tubes with radial fins. They found that the reactor took 490 s to reach
1.2 wt.% saturation at 15 bar supply pressure and 15 ◦ C HTF tempera­
ture. Dhaou et al. [26] carried out an experimental study on reactors
containing 1 kg of LaNi5 alloy. Two reactor designs, namely a simple
reactor with an external jacket and a reactor with a spiral finned tube,
were compared. It was observed that the absorption time dropped by
72.9% by using the spiral finned tube within the reactor. Ma et al. [28]
conducted an optimization study on a multi tube finned reactor. The
impact of variations in fin numbers, thickness, and radius was studied,
and it was concluded that the optimized reactor design took 1720 s for
complete absorption at 1 MPa gas pressure. Chandra et al. [29]
numerically investigated a multi tube reactor attached to conical fins.
They compared the effects of varying the tube and fins on reactor per­
formance and concluded that the multi tube reactor having 6 tubes
attached to 19 fins absorbed 80% and 90% of hydrogen in 290 s and 375
s at a gas pressure of 15 bar and a HTF temperature of 20 ◦ C. Recently, a
few studies reported advanced heat exchange structures. Wang et al.
[39] conducted an optimization study on a novel disc mini channel
reactor containing 1.56 kg of LaNi5 powder. Five reactor designs were
studied and it was found that this novel reactor saved 89% and 93% of
the time during the absorption and desorption processes compared to a
straight tube reactor. Bei et al. [40] numerically investigated a novel
reactor attached to tree shaped fins. The results revealed that this
reactor design saves 20.7% of absorption time compared to a radial
finned reactor.
In the realm of large-capacity hydrogen storage, the shell and tube
geometry have been extensively employed by numerous researchers.
Raju et al. [41] developed the design methodology to optimize the
cooling tubes for 50 kg capacity ECT reactors. Jana et al. [42] investi­
gated a tube bundle reactor (TBR) with a 25 kg alloy capacity. They
compared the performance of the TBR with the ECTR and found both
reactors took 10 min to reach 1.24 wt.% capacity. Also, the weight ratio
of the TBR was 1.17 times higher. The details of recently reported large
capacity reactors for hydrogen storage are listed in Table 1.

studies reported related to the design of the heat exchanger for hydrogen
storage systems. However, it’s worth noting that the majority of these
studies were conducted on a laboratory scale. For large capacity sta­
tionary hydrogen storage, both the overall reactor mass and perfor­
mance are of prime importance. The reported studies on large capacity

Reactor design and numerical implementation
Design description
The prime objective of this study is to design a large capacity MH
reactor targeting stationary hydrogen storage applications. The inspi­
ration behind this work is to develop a design approach that effectively
optimizes the reactor’s heat transfer performance by considering crucial
factors including size, weight, strength, and cost. The present study
proposes the shell and tube type cylindrical geometry, which consists of
multi tubes attached with disc fins surrounded by metallic powder inside
a shell with an external jacket, as shown in Fig. 1b. The shell diameter of
88.9 mm with a thickness of 3.2 mm is chosen to endure a maximum
pressure of 95 bar, and the reactor length of 1 m is adopted to accom­
modate ~20 kg of the alloy. The geometrical details of the reactor are
represented in Fig. 1c. The HTF tube diameter of 12.7 mm with a
thickness of 1.65 mm is optimally chosen by considering the heat
transfer area in mind. To ensure the compactness and light weight of the
MH reactor, a total of six HTF tubes is selected by considering the
minimum mass ratio of 1.5, given in Table 2.
The reactor mass encompasses the shell, HTF tubes, filter, hydrogen
tube, external jacket, end plates, and extended surfaces. The front and
back end plates having a thickness of 10 mm are employed to seal the
reactor. A 6 mm thickness is provided between the shell and external
jacket to facilitate the HTF flow. A porous filter having an outer diameter
of 9.5 mm is fitted at the center of the reactor for hydrogen exchange.
The optimized design consists of 94-disc type extended surfaces, each
with a thickness of 0.5 mm and a pitch of 10.5 mm, have been affixed
along the axis to improve heat transfer within the reactor. Since, heat
transfer is the crucial factor in MH systems [8], different MH reactor
designs (similar to the proposed reactor, the only difference is the type of
extended surface used within the reactor) are analyzed to determine the
most efficient reactor design, as shown in Fig. 1a.
Material selection
In this study, LaNi5 is chosen as the metal hydride alloy to facilitate a
more straightforward comparison with existing literature. Additionally,
this alloy demonstrates lower hysteresis in comparison to other AB5
alloys. Table 3 presents the thermochemical properties of the alloy and
hydrogen gas used for simulation. Freely accessible water having mod­
erate thermal conductivity and exceptional stability is selected as HTF.
For medium pressure applications, readily accessible ASTM (A-321)
standard seamless tubes composed of SS-316 material are used to design
the reactor. Copper is used as the material for fins because of its higher
thermal conductivity and outstanding corrosion resistant property.

During the absorption half cycle, the inlet hydrogen gas pressure and
cooling fluid temperature are set at 15 bar and 25 ◦ C, respectively, for all
the simulated models. Initially, there is a significant pressure difference,
which is the difference between the gas pressure and the equilibrium
pressure of the reactor, which results in rapid hydrogen absorption by
the metallic alloy. However, the heat is produced inside the reactor as a
result of hydrogen being absorbed by the metallic alloy. This heat causes
an immediate rise in the reactor’s temperature, which exponentially
increases the reactor’s equilibrium pressure, as per Eq. (11). As a result,
the pressure difference starts dropping, which slows down the rate of
absorption. Finally, the pressure difference becomes zero, and the
hydrogen absorption half cycle is completed. The average reactor tem­
perature and hydrogen absorbed (wt.%) for all models are represented
in Fig. 5a. It is observed that all models show similar performance
initially and absorb a weight percentage (wt.%) of 0.32 in the first 10 s.
Later, it is observed that the DFMTR model absorbs hydrogen at a faster
rate than other models. The DFMTR model absorbs the maximum stor­
age capacity, i.e.,1.37 wt.% of hydrogen in 795 s, which is 46%, 30%,
28%, and 27% faster than the SMTR, SFMTR, TFMTR, and LFMTR
models. Also, the maximum temperature of 72 ◦ C is reached by all the
models in just 32 s after absorption starts, as shown in Fig. 5a. It is seen
that the drop in the bed temperature of the DFMTR model is very fast
due to improved heat transfer, and the bed took 995 s to return to the
initial temperature of the HTF, whereas the LFMTR model, TFMTR
model, SFMTR model, and SMTR model take 1404 s, 1438 s, 1452 s, and
1928 s, respectively. However, the heat transfer rate at the end of the
absorption or desorption reactions was extremely slow; therefore, the
absorption process time was considered to be the time required to reach
the initial temperature within a temperature tolerance of 0.2 ◦ C, i.e.
25.2 ◦ C. The time required to reach the cooling fluid temperature by
DFMTR is reduced by 1.93 times as compared to SMTR.
The desorption half cycle encompasses the hydrogen discharge
process. In the present study, the hydrogen is discharged to the atmo­
sphere (1 bar pressure) at a heating fluid temperature of 50 ◦ C. When the
process initiates, the discharge rate is very high due to the higher
pressure difference between the reactor’s equilibrium and atmosphere
pressures. Also, there is a sudden drop in reactor temperature due to the
endothermic nature of the process, which reduces the pressure differ­
ence; therefore, a reduction in the discharge rate is observed. Later, the
reactor temperature starts increasing by taking heat from the heating
fluid and finally reaches the initial conditions. Fig. 5b shows the time

behavior of average reactor temperature and hydrogen desorbed (wt.%)
for all models. Initially, all models behaved similarly and discharged
0.15 wt.% of hydrogen in the first 10 s.
However, in a later stage, the DFMTR model was found to be superior
and discharges all the stored hydrogen in 1060 s, whereas the SMTR,
SFMTR, TFMTR, and LFMTR models take 2245 s, 1622 s, 1494 s, and
1439 s, respectively. The lowest temperature attained by all models is
16.5 ◦ C in the initial 60 s. The DFMTR model takes 1317 s to reach the
heating fluid temperature, which is 48%, 33%, 27%, and 24% less than
the SMTR, SFMTR, TFMTR, and LFMTR models. Similar to the absorp­
tion process, the desorption process time was considered to be the time
required to reach the initial temperature within a temperature tolerance
of 0.2 ◦ C, i.e. 49.8 ◦ C. The percentage reduction in time of the absorption
and desorption half cycles to absorb or desorb 90% of hydrogen for all
models compared to the baseline model is represented in Fig. 5c. It is
concluded from the above discussion that the large capacity DFMTR
model is efficient and saves 38% and 31% of the time for absorbing and
desorbing 90% of hydrogen compared to the baseline model. However,
the geometrical parameters inside the reactor should be in an optimized
position to achieve excellent performance. Therefore, it would be
worthwhile to do more research to observe the distribution of extended
surfaces inside the reactor.
The different cross-sectional cuts along the axial direction represent
the temperature distribution within the reactors, as shown in Fig. 6. The
cooler regions (blue regions) and hotter regions (red regions) signify the
low and high temperature regions inside the MH reactors. The pressure
difference is higher at lower bed temperatures during hydrogen storage
and higher at high temperatures during hydrogen release. Therefore, the
cooler regions (blue regions) and hotter regions (red regions) in Fig. 6
show excellent performance during the absorption and desorption pro­

occur only in the radial direction for each reactor, while there is a
uniform temperature distribution along the axial direction. During the
absorption process, it is observed that in the case of SMTR, hotter re­
gions are more visible, whereas in the case of DFMTR, cooler regions are
more visible compared to the other reactors due to the excellent heat
transfer provided by the disc fins. Therefore, DFMTR demonstrates su­
perior performance among all the reactors during the absorption pro­
cess. Similarly, during the desorption process, cooler regions are more
visible in SMTR, while regions closer to hotter regions are observed in
DFMTR, as shown in Fig. 6b. Therefore, the predicted performance of
DFMTR is found to be superior during the desorption process as well.
