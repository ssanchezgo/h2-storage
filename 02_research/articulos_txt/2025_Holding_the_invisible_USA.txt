ABSTRACT
Hydrogen storage remains the main barrier to the broader use of hydrogen as an energy carrier, despite
hydrogen’s high energy density and clean combustion. This study presents a comparative evaluation of
conventional and emerging storage methods, integrating thermodynamic, kinetic, economic, and environmental
metrics to assess capacity, efficiency, cost, and reversibility. Physisorption analysis reveals that metal organic
frameworks can achieve storage capacities up to 14.0 mmol/g. Chemical storage systems are evaluated, including nanostructured MgH2 (7.6 wt%), catalyzed reversible complex hydrides, liquid organic hydrogen carriers,
and clathrate hydrates. Techno-economic analysis shows storage costs from $500–700/kg H2 to $30–50/kg H2 ,
with energy efficiencies of 50%–90%. Life cycle assessment identifies manufacturing as the primary source
of emissions, with carbon footprints varying from 150 to 2057 kg CO2 -eq/kg H2 . Cryo-compressed is the most
practical transportation option while metal hydrides suit stationary use. This study provides a quantitative
foundation to guide material selection and system design for next-generation hydrogen storage technologies.

1. Introduction
The global transition toward cleaner and more sustainable energy
sources has elevated hydrogen as a promising energy carrier. Its unique
characteristics, including abundance, high energy content per unit
mass, and environmentally benign combustion (producing only water
vapor), make it a valuable energy resource [1]. Hydrogen is anticipated
to play a crucial role across multiple sectors, including transportation,
energy storage, and industrial processes over the next few decades [2].
Recent data from the International Energy Agency (IEA) highlights
hydrogen’s expanding role in the energy sector, as seen in Fig. 1 [3].
In 2023, global hydrogen demand reached a record 97 Mt, representing a 2.5% increase from 2022, driven primarily by refining and
chemical industries. This demonstrates the potential for scale-up to
49 Mt by 2030, representing a 30% increase from previous projections. Electrolysis is emerging as a critical technology, with global
electrolyzer capacity expected to reach 520 GW by 2030 [3]. However,
the practical implementation of hydrogen technologies faces significant
challenges, particularly in hydrogen storage. Storage systems must
simultaneously meet rigorous criteria, including high gravimetric and
volumetric capacities, cost-effectiveness, rapid kinetic performance,
and operational safety [4,5]. The low energy density of hydrogen at
standard conditions necessitates innovative storage solutions to enable widespread adoption. Storage strategies range from small-scale
options (e.g., pressurized tanks for vehicles) to large-scale underground
hydrogen storage in geological formations that can buffer seasonal
supply–demand variations [6,7]. Underground storage in salt caverns
or depleted gas reservoirs provides massive capacity and capitalizes on
existing subsurface infrastructure, making it a key component for bulk
storage needs in a future hydrogen economy.
Recognizing these challenges, the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE)
has established progressive targets for hydrogen storage systems to
guide research and development efforts. These targets, detailed in Table
1, demonstrate a strategic approach to improving hydrogen storage
technologies across multiple performance parameters, including gravimetric capacity, hydrogen content, volumetric capacity, and system
operational characteristics.
This paper provides a comprehensive review of hydrogen storage technologies, systematically examining physical storage methods
(including adsorption on porous materials) versus chemical storage
methods, which involves hydrogen bonding with other materials and
released on-demand. Section 2 discusses physical hydrogen storage,
including compressed gas, cryogenic liquid, cryo-compressed hydrogen,
and novel physisorption techniques using porous materials. Section 3
covers chemical storage, such as metal hydrides (and their advanced
nanostructured variants), complex hydrides, and Liquid Organic Hydrogen Carriers LOHCs (with an emphasis on formic acid). Section 5
provides an in-depth summary of the discussed methods and Sections 6
and 7 dive into techno-economic considerations and life cycle assessments of the various methods. Finally the conclusion identifies research
gaps and the future outlook.

2.1. Compressed hydrogen storage
Compressed hydrogen storage involves storing hydrogen gas at high
pressures, typically between 350 and 700 bar, in tanks constructed from
advanced materials such as metals or composite polymers to ensure
structural integrity under stress [2]. This method is technologically
mature and widely deployed due to its operational simplicity and rapid
refueling capabilities, which are critical for automotive applications.
Modern storage systems use tank designs categorized as Type I (allmetal), Type II (metal liner with composite hoop wrapping), Type III
(metal liner fully wrapped with composite), and Type IV (polymer
liner fully wrapped with composite). Type IV tanks are lightweight and
minimize hydrogen permeation, though they require careful material
selection to ensure durability over repeated pressure cycles [8].
Despite their advantages, compressed systems face challenges, including significant energy consumption during compression (10%–15%
of the hydrogen’s energy content) and high costs linked to carbon fiber
composites [5]. Additionally, hydrogen’s low volumetric energy density limits practicality in space-constrained applications like light-duty
vehicles.
Safety concerns, such as risks of leaks or tank ruptures, necessitate
robust engineering solutions, including advanced valve systems and
compliance with rigorous safety standards [9]. Recent advancements
focus on improving composite materials to enhance gravimetric capacity and reduce environmental impacts. For instance, novel carbon
fiber composites and polymer liners have improved impermeability and
resistance to hydrogen embrittlement [8]. Hybrid approaches, such as
cryo-compressed storage (combining moderate compression with subambient subambient temperatures) and systems integrating hydrogen
with other energy carriers like compressed air, aim to optimize efficiency and volumetric capacity [10,11]. Ongoing research, guided
by DOE targets, emphasizes innovations in additive manufacturing,
advanced resins, and pressure-regulation technologies to reduce costs
and improve performance. However, achieving cost-competitive commercial deployment will require further breakthroughs in material
science and infrastructure development.
Advancements in materials science are focused on developing
stronger, lighter materials for tank construction, such as advanced
fiber-reinforced composites, to improve gravimetric capacities and
reduce environmental impacts. Additionally, research into alternative
tank designs and conformable storage systems aims to optimize space
utilization in vehicles [12]. Contemporary studies are also exploring
hybrid storage concepts, for instance, combining compressed hydrogen with other energy storage media. Notably, Bartela (2020) [10]
proposed a hybrid energy storage system using compressed air and
hydrogen to reduce energy consumption and improve efficiency. Nevertheless, the main drawbacks remain the high material costs and
energy consumption for compression, along with the relatively low
energy density of hydrogen compared to conventional fuels [13,14].
Ongoing research seeks to address these issues through innovative composite materials and advanced pressure regulation systems, including
the development of different tank types (Type I–IV) with improved
performance characteristics [14].

2.3. Cryo-compressed hydrogen storage

2.4. Refrigeration system optimization for mixed cryogenic compression

Cryo-compressed hydrogen storage is a hybrid approach that merges
cryogenic cooling with high-pressure compression, offering a middle
ground between liquid and high-pressure gas storage. Unlike liquid
hydrogen stored near atmospheric pressure or gaseous hydrogen at
ambient temperature and high pressure, this method utilizes subcooled
hydrogen at elevated pressures to enhance storage efficiency [24]. By
partially lowering the temperature, storage pressure can be reduced
while maintaining a higher density than conventional compressed gas
storage [25]. This approach mitigates boil-off losses associated with
liquid hydrogen while improving volumetric energy density beyond
that of high-pressure gas storage [26]. In cryo-compressed systems,
hydrogen is typically cooled to temperatures anywhere between 2̃0
K and 80 K and then compressed to pressures that can reach around
20–35 MPa [27]. The combination of reduced temperature and moderate pressure substantially elevates hydrogen density in the storage
vessel. The storage tank in a cryo-compressed system is usually a
vacuum-insulated vessel (similar to a liquid hydrogen Dewar) but also
reinforced to withstand significant internal pressure [26]. One can
broadly identify variations in design for cryo-compressed tanks: (i)
single-walled vessels with very thick insulation designed to minimize
heat transfer under moderate pressures, and (ii) multi-layer, composite
tanks using advanced carbon-fiber composites to handle the combined
stresses of low temperatures and pressurization [25].
Cryo-compressed storage offers longer dormancy, the duration before venting becomes necessary, and achieves higher overall hydrogen
densities than either cryogenic or compressed storage alone. Dormancy
times can reach weeks under optimized conditions, and hydrogen densities exceeding 40 g H2 ∕L have been reported, surpassing conventional
700 bar tanks. [26]. This hybrid approach also lowers the effective
boil-off: because the tank can tolerate some pressure increase, evaporating hydrogen can be kept in the tank until it is re-cooled or used,
rather than vented. Recent research has explored strategies to enhance
cryo-compressed system performance. For instance, incorporating a
throttling valve for hydrogen venting with cooling recovery can reduce
pressure swings and reclaim cooling power from vented gas [28].

by maintaining hydrogen in a supercritical state at cryogenic temperatures (70–80 K) and moderate pressures (30–50 MPa). This approach
achieves storage densities comparable to liquid hydrogen while eliminating the need for constant energy input to sustain the liquid phase.
Recent optimization studies have demonstrated significant improvements in storage efficiency through refrigeration systems, pressure
regulation strategies, and integrated system designs.
The most effective refrigeration optimization approaches combine
liquid nitrogen pre-cooling with specialized expansion cycles using
optimized working fluids. Xu et al. [30] demonstrated that mixed
refrigerant systems coupled with gas expansion cycles achieve specific
energy consumption as low as 5.87 kWh kg−1 H2 when using neon as
the expansion working fluid, representing nearly 50% of the theoretical
minimum of 2.92 kWh kg−1 H2 . Comparative analysis shows neon
outperforms helium (6.15 kWh kg−1 H2 ) and hydrogen (6.42 kWh
kg−1 H2 ) in expansion cycles due to superior thermophysical properties
and heat transfer characteristics. Liquid nitrogen pre-cooling systems
provide effective intermediate cooling before final refrigeration, typically reducing overall energy consumption by 20%–25% compared to
systems relying solely on mechanical refrigeration. Song et al. [31]
achieved storage densities of 72.53 kg m−3 at 70 K and 50 MPa
using helium expansion cycles with liquid nitrogen pre-cooling, demonstrating specific energy consumption of 6.2 kWh kg−1 H2 and exergy
efficiency of 47.2%. Heat exchanger network optimization using pinch
analysis techniques further improves thermal efficiency by 10%–15%
through enhanced heat integration and reduced irreversibilities.
Optimal pressure regulation strategies use multi-stage compression
with pressure ratios of 3–4 per stage, minimizing compression work
while maintaining acceptable discharge temperatures. Variable pressure operation in the 30–50 MPa range enables dynamic optimization
based on operating conditions, achieving energy savings of 8%–12%
compared to fixed pressure systems. Advanced control systems incorporate real-time thermodynamic property calculations and predictive

The systematic optimization of mixed cryogenic compressed hydrogen storage systems follows a structured approach that addresses
each major system component and their interactions. The first step involves comprehensive system modeling and simulation using advanced
process simulation software such as Aspen HYSYS or CHEMCAD to
establish baseline performance characteristics and identify optimization
opportunities. The modeling process must accurately represent the thermodynamic properties of hydrogen at cryogenic conditions, including
real gas effects, phase behavior, and transport properties.
The second optimization step focuses on component-level optimization to maximize the performance of individual system elements before
addressing system-level interactions. Compressor optimization involves
the selection of optimal compression ratios, intercooling strategies, and
capacity control methods to minimize energy consumption while maintaining required pressure levels. Heat exchanger optimization addresses
heat transfer enhancement, pressure drop minimization, and thermal
effectiveness maximization through advanced design techniques and
materials selection. Refrigeration system optimization evaluates different cycle configurations, working fluid selections, and operating
conditions to achieve maximum cooling capacity with minimum energy
input.
The third step implements system-level optimization that considers the interactions and trade-offs between different components and
subsystems. Multi-objective optimization algorithms, such as genetic algorithms or particle swarm optimization, systematically evaluate thousands of potential system configurations to identify optimal design

and operating parameters. The optimization process typically considers 25–30 key variables simultaneously, including compression ratios,
refrigerant compositions, heat exchanger configurations, and operating
temperatures and pressures.
The fourth step involves dynamic optimization that addresses system performance under varying operating conditions and transient
scenarios. Dynamic optimization considers factors such as startup and
shutdown procedures, load-following capabilities, and response to disturbances or equipment failures. The development of optimal control strategies ensures that the system maintains peak performance
across all operating modes while providing adequate safety margins
and operational flexibility.
The final optimization step focuses on economic optimization that
balances performance improvements with capital and operating cost
considerations. Life cycle cost analysis evaluates the total cost of ownership including capital costs, operating expenses, maintenance requirements, and end-of-life considerations. The economic optimization
process identifies the optimal balance between performance and cost,
ensuring that optimization efforts focus on improvements that provide the greatest economic benefit. The most advanced optimized
systems achieve storage densities of 72.53 kg m−3 with specific energy
consumption approaching 5.87 kWh kg−1 H2 and exergy efficiencies
exceeding 49%, demonstrating substantial improvements over conventional storage methods and the potential for mixed cryogenic systems
to provide an optimal balance between storage density and energy
efficiency for large-scale hydrogen storage applications.
2.5. Physical adsorption in porous materials
Physisorption-based hydrogen storage relies on the adsorption of
hydrogen onto porous materials at cryogenic temperatures (∼ 77 K).
This approach stores hydrogen at relatively low pressures by exploiting the large surface areas of adsorbent materials [33]. Commonly
studied adsorbents include carbon-based nanostructures, metal–organic
frameworks (MOFs), covalent organic frameworks (COFs), and novel
host materials like clathrate hydrates. Although inherently safer due to
their low-pressure operation and modular design, these systems require
cryogenic cooling because hydrogen exhibits weak adsorption energies
on these surfaces, limiting effective storage at higher temperatures.
2.5.1. Carbon-based materials
Carbon materials, including activated carbons, carbon nanotubes
(CNTs), fullerenes, and graphene, have been extensively investigated
for hydrogen storage due to their high surface areas, tunable pore
structures, and chemical stability [34–36]. Early studies reported exceptionally high hydrogen uptake in certain carbon nanostructures, for
instance, Dillon et al. (1997) [37] claimed hydrogen storage capacity
of approximately 5–7 wt% in CNTs at ambient conditions. However,
subsequent research revealed that achievable adsorption capacities are
closely linked to specific surface area and pore size distribution, and
that initial high values were not reproducible [38,39]. Typically, there
is about 1 wt% hydrogen storage per 500 m2 ∕g of surface area at 77
K, known as the Chahine rule. This implies that ultra-high surface area
carbons (3000 m2 ∕g or more) are necessary to approach the DOE targets
via physisorption, achievable only at cryogenic temperatures.
Enhancing hydrogen uptake in carbon materials has been focused on
structural tailoring, including templating for ordered porosity, chemical
activation to increase micropore volume, and heteroatom or metal
doping to strengthen hydrogen interactions [40–42]. For example,
activated carbon derived from biomass waste can achieve surface areas
above 3000 m2 ∕g and show hydrogen capacities ∼ 6 wt% at 77 K and
40 bar [43]. However, practical applications of carbon adsorbents are
limited by the need for cryogenic temperatures (typically liquid nitrogen temperature, 77 K) to achieve significant hydrogen uptake, and by
challenges in producing materials with consistently high performance.

2.5.3. Covalent organic frameworks (COFs) and polymers
COFs and porous organic polymers (such as Conjugated Microporous Polymers (CMPs), Hyper-Crosslinked Polymers (HCPs), Porous

with high surface areas and low densities [55]. They provide tunable
pore sizes, structural diversity, and lightweight nature, making them
promising for hydrogen storage applications [56,57]. Similar to MOFs,
strategies to enhance hydrogen storage in COFs involve increasing
surface area, optimizing pore size (to maximize overlap of adsorption
potentials), and incorporating functional groups or dopants to enhance
hydrogen interactions [58,59]. For example, porous aromatic frameworks doped with exposed metal sites have been studied to increase

2.5.4. Adsorption energy and temperature effects in porous materials
Optimizing hydrogen storage in porous materials requires tuning
adsorption energies through complementary strategies. The ideal binding range, 15–25 kJ mol−1 , balances ambient-temperature retention
with efficient desorption, well above typical physisorption energies
(4–8 kJ mol−1 ) in pristine materials. Open metal sites offer a direct
route to boosting adsorption energies. Varying metal centers in isostructural MOFs like M2 (dobdc) (MOF-74 analogs) shows clear trends: Mn2+
(22.1), Fe2+ (19.8), Co2+ (17.2), Ni2+ (15.1), Cu2+ (12.8), and Zn2+
(8.9 kJ mol−1 ), with binding strength tied to metal charge density
and electronic structure [61]. This allows precise tuning via metal
selection. Matching pore size to the kinetic diameter of H2 (2.89 Å)
improves adsorption by enhancing the overlap of van der Waals potentials from opposing pore walls. Pores in the 6–12 Årange, particularly
those around 7–8 Å, exhibit up to 40%–60% higher binding energies.
This effect is more pronounced in slit-shaped pores than in cylindrical
geometries [62].
Ligand functionalization offers a versatile approach for tuning local
binding environments by introducing polar groups, heteroatoms, or
pendant metal sites. For example, amine-functionalized MOFs such as
mmen-Mg2 (dobpdc) achieve binding energies of 21–25 kJ mol−1 through
weak coordination between hydrogen and nitrogen lone pairs. Lithium
incorporation via post-synthetic modification can also yield binding
energies of 18–22 kJ mol−1 while preserving structural stability and
reversibility [63]. Controlling framework interpenetration provides another means of optimizing adsorption. Non-interpenetrated structures
tend to have larger pores but weaker interactions, whereas tailored
interpenetration can produce ideal pore dimensions with enhanced
adsorption potentials. This parameter can be systematically adjusted
through ligand design and synthesis conditions [64]. The practical
viability of porous hydrogen storage materials depends heavily on
their performance from cryogenic (77 K) to ambient (298 K) conditions. Temperature-dependent analysis is essential for identifying performance limits and refining material design. At 77 K, top-performing
MOFs approach theoretical storage limits. MOF-210 achieves a total
uptake of 17.6 wt% at 80 bar, and NU-1501 reaches 14.0 wt% under
similar conditions. These capacities reflect near-complete pore filling,
as storage is limited by available pore volume rather than binding
strength under cryogenic conditions [65]. (See Table 2.)

2.5.5. Clathrate hydrates
Clathrate hydrates are crystalline, ice-like solids where hydrogen
molecules are physically trapped within the cavities formed by a lattice
of water molecules [69]. They have the potential for hydrogen storage
at near-ambient temperatures and pressures, as the water lattice stabilizes the hydrogen, making them attractive for practical applications
if their storage capacity can be improved [70]. Traditional clathrate
hydrates of hydrogen, however, have very low hydrogen storage capacities (< 0.5 wt% typically) and require high formation pressures (often
100–200 MPa for pure hydrogen hydrates), along with slow kinetics of
formation and release [71]. These factors have historically made them
impractical.
Su et al. (2008) [72] developed a method to enhance hydrogen
clathrate formation using an ultralow-density emulsion-templated polymer (polyHIPE foam) as a scaffold [72]. The macroporous structure
with interconnected open cells provided abundant nucleation sites and
short diffusion pathways, significantly improving hydrate formation
kinetics. This approach achieved a hydrogen storage capacity of 0.18
wt% at 270 K (just below room temperature), with 90% of that capacity
reached within 60 min, far surpassing conventional hydrate formation
rates. However, this capacity remains orders of magnitude below the
DOE target (> 5 wt%).

2.6. Summary and limitations of physical storage methods

3.1. Nanostructured materials

The physical hydrogen storage methods covered here are the most
mature, each with specific strengths and specific trade-offs. Compressed

suffers from boil-off losses. Cryo-compressed storage balances density
and dormancy but adds system complexity and infrastructure demands.

hydrogen uptake and release through spillover mechanisms [109].
Through these nanostructuring and composite approaches, substantial
improvements have been achieved, with many hydrides now exhibiting lower hydrogen release temperatures and significantly enhanced
kinetics. However, challenges remain in scaling up nanoscale hydrides
while ensuring long-term stability and safety. The potential for pyrophoric behavior in nanopowders and the high cost of nanoscale
processing must be addressed. Despite these hurdles, ongoing advancements in nanostructured hydrides represent important steps toward
making metal hydrides a viable hydrogen storage solution for practical
applications [110].
Another critical aspect is the stability of metal hydrides under

thermal conductivity and hydrogen transport kinetics [113]. Cycle

1500–2000 cycles at moderate temperatures (20–60 ◦ C), while MgH2 based systems show more pronounced degradation (10%–20% capacity

3.2. Metal hydrides
Metal hydrides store hydrogen by forming solid compounds (interstitial or complex phases) where hydrogen is absorbed into the metal
lattice, offering high volumetric hydrogen densities and safe, relatively
low-pressure storage [93]. Examples include interstitial hydrides like
magnesium hydride (MgH2 ), which has a high theoretical storage capacity (∼ 7.6 wt%), and complex alanates like sodium alanate (NaAlH4 )
or simple saline hydrides like lithium hydride (LiH). Typically, hydrogen absorption/desorption in metal hydrides occurs at moderate
pressures (under 10 MPa) and, for some systems, near ambient temperatures, making them attractive for stationary storage or certain
vehicular applications where weight is less critical [94].
Metal hydrides offer a promising approach to hydrogen storage, providing high volumetric densities and safe, compact storage. However,
many hydrides require elevated temperatures to release hydrogen. For
instance, MgH2 desorbs hydrogen appreciably only above ∼ 300 ◦ C,
posing challenges for onboard storage in vehicles [95]. Doping with
transition metal catalysts can enhance kinetics and reduce desorption temperatures. A well-known example is NaAlH4 , which, when
doped with Ti-based catalysts, enables reversible hydrogen storage at
∼ 120 ◦ C, generating significant research interest [96]. Despite these
advancements, metal hydrides still face limitations related to operating
temperature, kinetics, stability, and practical storage capacities.
High operating temperatures remain a major drawback, with many
complex hydrides requiring temperatures above 250 ◦ C and MgH2 exceeding 300 ◦ C. Effective heat management is necessary for practical
implementation. Additionally, without catalysts, absorption and desorption kinetics are slow due to hydrogen diffusion and crystal structure rearrangement. Repeated hydrogenation/dehydrogenation cycles
can lead to particle coarsening or phase segregation, reducing capacity
over time. For example, MgH2 particles tend to sinter, further slowing
kinetics upon cycling [97]. While volumetric capacity is high, gravimetric capacity remains a limitation, as many metal hydrides have modest
hydrogen storage capacities (< 2–3 wt%) due to the significant mass of
the metal. Only select hydrides, such as MgH2 and AlH3 , exceed 5 wt%,
often under impractical conditions. Furthermore, some hydrides rely on
expensive or heavy elements, such as LaNi5 in NiMH batteries, which
increases costs and limits large-scale adoption in transportation [98].
To address these challenges, research has focused on modifying
metal hydrides through nanostructuring and catalysis. Nanostructuring
enhances hydrogen absorption and desorption kinetics by increasing
surface area and reducing hydrogen diffusion distances. Paskevicius
et al. [99] demonstrated that ∼7 nm MgH2 nanoparticles exhibit a
reduction in decomposition enthalpy of 2.84 kJ mol−1 H2 compared
to bulk MgH2 (𝛥𝐻nano = 71.22 ± 0.49 kJ mol−1 H2 vs. 𝛥𝐻bulk = 74.06 ±
0.42 kJ mol−1 H2 ), along with a reduction in reaction entropy (𝛥𝑆nano =
129.6 ± 0.8 J mol−1 H2 K −1 vs. 𝛥𝑆bulk = 133.4 ± 0.7 J mol−1 H2 K −1 ). This
thermodynamic alteration results in a lower hydrogen equilibrium
desorption temperature at 1 bar, with 𝑇1 bar reduced by ∼ 6 ◦ C to
276.2 ± 2.4 ◦ C compared to bulk MgH2 at 281.8 ± 2.2 ◦ C.
Additionally, it can alter thermodynamics, potentially lowering the
temperature required for hydrogen release [100]. For instance, MgH2

3.2.2. Kinetic enhancement mechanisms
Nanostructuring provides dramatic kinetic improvements through
multiple pathways including reduced diffusion distances, increased
surface-to-volume ratios, and enhanced nucleation site density. The
quantitative relationship between particle size and kinetic performance
follows power law scaling relationships that enable predictive design of
fast-kinetics systems. (See Fig. 9.)
Hydrogen sorption kinetics in metal hydrides are strongly sizedependent. The absorption rate constant follows 𝑘abs ∝ 𝑑 −1.5 , and
desorption scales as 𝑘des ∝ 𝑑 −1.2 , where 𝑑 is the particle diameter [120].
These relationships predict substantial rate enhancements, up to 100×
for absorption and 30× for desorption, when particle sizes are reduced
from 100,nm to 5,nm. This improvement is attributed to reduced
diffusion lengths and higher surface-area-to-volume ratios. In bulk
MgH2 , hydrogen diffusion through thick hydride layers limits reaction
rates, resulting in apparent activation energies of 160–180,kJ,mol−1 .
Nanoconfinement lowers these values to 110–140,kJ,mol−1 and increases pre-exponential factors by 2–3 orders of magnitude due to a
higher density of accessible active sites [121]. Temperature-dependent

3.2.3. Nanostructure–catalyst synergy in metal hydrides
The integration of nanostructuring and catalytic doping has significantly advanced metal hydride performance, with synergistic effects
that surpass the contributions of either approach alone. This strategy
has been particularly effective in enabling reversible hydrogen storage
at practical temperatures with improved cycling stability. A key example is the titanium-doped sodium alanate (NaAlH4 ) system, where
Ti-based catalysis combined with nanoscale dispersion has produced
near-commercial performance metrics [125].
In Ti-doped NaAlH4 , the catalytic mechanism involves a progressive
transformation of Ti species during cycling: Ti3+ → amorphous Ti0
→ AlTi phases → Al3 Ti intermetallics. These transformations result in
uniformly dispersed active sites, ∼30 nm in size, embedded within
the aluminum matrix. These sites catalyze hydrogen dissociation and
facilitate spillover processes. Rehydrogenation proceeds via (1) H2
dissociation on Ti sites, (2) spillover and alane (Al𝑥 H𝑦 ) formation,
and (3) reconstruction of NaAlH4 . This mechanistic insight provides a
framework for designing more active and stable catalyst systems [125].
Combining nanostructuring with Ti-based catalysis yields performance
gains well beyond those achieved by either method alone. Nanostructured MgH2 can lower dehydrogenation temperatures by 100–150 °C,
while Ti-catalyzed bulk NaAlH4 enables reversible storage near 120 °C.

3.3. Complex hydrides
Complex hydrides are chemical compounds where hydrogen is
bonded in complex anions (such as [BH4 ]− , [AlH4 ]− ) often paired
with light cations. They offer some of the highest known gravimetric
hydrogen capacities. For example, lithium borohydride (LiBH4 ) contains 18.5 wt% hydrogen (theoretical) and ammonia borane (NH3 BH3 )
∼ 19.6 wt%. The allure of complex hydrides is tempered by their
thermodynamic stability and kinetic sluggishness, which typically make
hydrogen release difficult [131].
A representative complex hydride is ammonia borane (NH3 BH3 ).
It releases hydrogen in a three-step decomposition starting around
110 ◦ C and finishing by ∼ 150 ◦ C (for ∼ 12 wt% H2 released), but the
spent material is a polyborazine polymer that is difficult to rehydrogenate [19]. Baitalow et al. [132] characterized the dehydrogenation
of NH3 BH3 in three distinct steps: first at 70–112 °C (release of the
first equivalent of H2 ), second at 110–150 °C (release of the second
equivalent), and third at >150 °C (release of the third equivalent).
While the theoretical hydrogen content is 19.6 wt%, approximately
12–13 wt% is practically accessible below 150 °C through the first two
steps. Kondrat’ev et al. [133] determined that the activation energy for
hydrogen evolution from solid NH3 BH3 is 94 ± 11 kJ mol−1 in the temperature range of 327–351 K, with a measured sublimation enthalpy
of 76.3 ± 3.0 kJ mol−1 at 357 K. Significant research has gone into
making ammonia borane more practical, such as using nano-confined
AB in porous hosts or reactive additives to promote complete hydrogen
release at lower temperatures, similar to the study by Chen et al.
(2024). Despite progress, AB is seen more as a one-time use chemical
hydrogen source (for fuel cells in portable power, for example) rather
than a rechargeable storage medium [134].
Metal Borohydrides (MBH4 ): LiBH4 , NaBH4 , Mg(BH4 )2 , etc., have
very high hydrogen content but very high stability. LiBH4 releases
hydrogen in multiple steps from 320 ◦ C upward, and direct rehydrogenation of LiH + B to LiBH4 requires > 600 ◦ C and > 35 MPa H2 , not
feasible in onboard systems. Doping and reactive hydride composites
have had some success (e.g., LiBH4 + MgH2 mixtures show lower
combined dehydrogenation temperature due to mutual destabilization).
Mg(BH4 )2 has attracted attention because it can reversibly release ∼
9–14 wt% H2 under 200–300 ◦ C with appropriate catalysts, but full
reversibility is still not achieved [135].

allowing for safe and efficient hydrogen transport in liquid form under
ambient conditions [141]. These compounds absorb hydrogen through
exothermic hydrogenation, forming saturated molecules, and later release it via endothermic dehydrogenation, regenerating the original

LOHCs store hydrogen chemically and release it when needed. Ideal
LOHCs should exhibit high hydrogen storage capacities, favorable thermodynamics and kinetics, chemical and thermal stability, low toxicity,
and economic viability [142].

to release hydrogen, with NEC being fully regenerated [141,143].

Toluene/methylcyclohexane (MCH) is another widely researched
LOHC system, where toluene is hydrogenated to MCH, storing approximately 6.1 wt% hydrogen. The reversible cycle between toluene and
MCH has been utilized in laboratory and industrial applications due to
its well-established production and handling infrastructure [93]. However, dehydrogenation of MCH requires temperatures around 300 ◦ C
and the use of noble metal catalysts, typically Pt- or Pd-based. More
recently, dibenzyltoluene (DBT) and its derivatives have gained attention for their suitability as LOHCs, offering a hydrogen capacity of
∼ 6.2 wt%. Ojelade & Zaman [145] conducted a comprehensive thermodynamic analysis of the methylcyclohexane–toluene system, demonstrating almost 100% equilibrium conversion of methylcyclohexane
with ≈99% H2 yield at 325 ◦ C and 1 bar during dehydrogenation. For
the reverse toluene hydrogenation, calculations predict near-complete
conversion at 100 ◦ C and 1 bar, though kinetic limitations in current catalysts prevent realizing these theoretical values at atmospheric
pressure. These compounds remain liquid in both hydrogen-lean and
hydrogen-rich states, possess low vapor pressure-which enhances safety
by reducing flammability risks-and exhibit strong thermal stability,
making them viable for large-scale applications [146].
LOHCs present several advantages in hydrogen storage and transport. They achieve high volumetric hydrogen densities comparable to
liquid hydrogen but without requiring cryogenic temperatures. Because
LOHCs remain in liquid form under ambient conditions, they can
be integrated into existing fuel storage and distribution infrastructure, reducing the need for new systems. In their hydrogen-lean form,
most LOHCs are non-flammable, non-explosive, and exhibit low vapor
pressure, minimizing safety concerns. Additionally, the reversibility of
the hydrogenation/dehydrogenation cycle allows LOHCs to be reused
multiple times, unlike one-time-use chemical hydrides [142].

Despite these advantages, several challenges must be addressed for
widespread adoption of LOHC technology. A major drawback is the
endothermic nature of dehydrogenation, which requires substantial
heat input, typically in the range of 150–300 ◦ C, depending on the
carrier [141]. Efficient heat management and external heat sourcessuch as waste heat recovery-are necessary for continuous operation.
Another limitation is the reliance on noble metal catalysts, such as Pd,
Pt, and Ru, to facilitate hydrogenation and dehydrogenation reactions.
These catalysts are expensive and susceptible to poisoning from sulfur
and other impurities, necessitating high-purity LOHCs. Research into
non-noble metal alternatives, such as Ni-based catalysts, is ongoing,
though noble metals currently provide superior performance [147].
The hydrogen release rate and overall efficiency also face challenges, particularly for applications requiring rapid hydrogen supply,
such as fuel cells. Reactor designs must optimize heat transfer and
catalysis to ensure sufficient hydrogen release while maintaining energy efficiency. The logistics of LOHC recycling further complicate
implementation, as the spent hydrogen-lean carrier must be transported
back to hydrogenation facilities, effectively requiring a closed-loop
supply chain [146]. This adds cost and complexity, as tanker trucks or
pipelines would need to carry hydrogen-rich LOHCs to fueling stations
and return with hydrogen-lean LOHCs for reprocessing. Additionally,
partial dehydrogenation and by-product formation can lead to multiple
hydrogenated species or intermediates, which complicate hydrogen
utilization and necessitate precise control of reaction conditions.
Ongoing research focuses on addressing these challenges through
improved catalysts and the discovery of new LOHC molecules with
optimized properties. Developing catalysts with higher activity and selectivity can lower the temperature required for dehydrogenation while
increasing hydrogen release rates. Exploring cheaper metal alternatives
or structured bimetallic catalysts may also reduce costs [146]. Another promising avenue is the design of LOHC molecules with slightly

is being explored to improve overall energy efficiency. For instance,
utilizing surplus heat from power plants to drive the hydrogen release process could significantly enhance the viability of LOHC-based

3.6. Photocatalytic and light-driven storage systems
Light-driven hydrogen storage systems offer the potential to directly
harness solar energy for hydrogen production and storage processes,
enabling fully sustainable energy cycles. Recent advances have shown
that photocatalytic materials can effectively drive hydrogen storage
and release reactions using only sunlight, removing the need for external energy inputs [156]. These systems typically rely on advanced
photocatalysts that convert light energy directly into chemical energy
for hydrogen liberation, with some materials now capable of utilizing near-infrared light to extend the usable solar spectrum beyond
traditional visible-light systems. The integration of photocatalytic processes with hydrogen storage materials has opened new possibilities
for distributed energy systems and off-grid applications. Advanced
photocatalysts demonstrate improved performance under real solar
conditions and hold promise for practical outdoor deployment [156].
The development of hybrid photocatalytic–storage systems allows continuous operation during daylight hours while storing hydrogen for
later use, creating a complete solar-to-hydrogen solution. These technologies are especially valuable for remote or developing regions where
access to conventional energy infrastructure is limited.

3.5.1. Formic acid as a hydrogen storage medium
Formic acid (HCOOH) functions as a specialized type of LOHC,
storing hydrogen in a simple liquid that decomposes into hydrogen
and carbon dioxide under relatively mild conditions [149]. Although its
hydrogen content is only 4.4 wt%, which is modest compared to other
hydrogen carriers, its primary advantage lies in the low-temperature
release of hydrogen, typically between 70 and 120 ◦ C with appropriate
catalysts. Additionally, formic acid remains a liquid under ambient
conditions, making it easy to handle and integrate into existing fuel
infrastructure.
Catalytic decomposition of formic acid has been extensively studied, demonstrating efficient hydrogen release. For instance, Czaun
et al. [150] generated the active catalyst in situ from RuCl3 ⋅𝑥H2 O and
triphenylphosphine in an SDS-stabilized toluene/water emulsion. At 90
◦ C they achieved complete conversion of formic acid to an equimolar
H2 /CO2 stream with no detectable CO; the initial rate (1.69 × 10−5 mol
L−1 s−1 with 2.43 mM Ru) corresponds to a turnover frequency of ≈25
h−1 , rising to ≈100 h−1 at 100 ◦ C.
A key challenge, however, is the production of CO2 as a byproduct.
If this CO2 can be captured or chemically recycled-such as through
hydrogenation back into formic acid using renewable hydrogen-the
overall process could become carbon-neutral. Filonenko et al. [151]
demonstrated a ruthenium-based catalyst capable of hydrogenating
CO2 to formic acid with a TON up to 1,100,000 and a TOF up to
23,000 h−1 at 120 ◦ C and 40 bar (H2 /CO2 = 1:1). However, the reaction
has 𝛥𝐺◦ = +32.9 kJ mol−1 at standard conditions, necessitating the use
of a base or other techniques to shift equilibrium for practical application. Recent research has focused on improving both decomposition and
synthesis catalysts, as well as investigating electrocatalytic pathways
for directly converting CO2 to formate using renewable electricity,
potentially simplifying the hydrogen storage cycle and enhancing its
environmental sustainability [152].
Recent advances in formic acid–based hydrogen storage have
brought the technology closer to practical application. Sang et al.
(2024) introduced a potassium formate/potassium bicarbonate system
that remained stable over six months and completed 40 full storage–
release cycles [153]. Using only trace amounts of ruthenium catalyst,
the system released hydrogen at a rate of 9.3 L/h with 99.5% purity and
achieved a potential storage efficiency of up to 67%. Operated under
mild conditions without additional chemical inputs, this carbon-neutral
approach stores hydrogen in benign salt solutions. Photocatalytic methods have also advanced significantly, with Peng et al. (2024) reporting
light-driven dehydrogenation of formic acid at activity levels 100
times higher than previous photocatalytic systems [154]. Their system operates at room temperature using sunlight, eliminating the
need for external heating. Notably, they demonstrated the coupling
of photocatalytic hydrogen generation with enzymatic synthesis to
produce high-value chemical and pharmaceutical compounds, marking
the first such integration of renewable hydrogen production with sustainable chemical manufacturing. Ongoing efforts continue to address
limitations by developing more efficient catalysts, refining decomposition mechanisms, and exploring advanced CO2 capture and recycling
strategies. These improvements are essential for transitioning formic
acid–based systems from a promising concept to an economically viable
component of hydrogen energy infrastructure. More broadly, LOHCs
remain a key research focus, with future directions aimed at reducing catalyst costs, optimizing carrier molecules, and integrating these
systems with renewable energy and waste heat recovery to improve
efficiency and overall sustainability [155].

6. Life cycle assessment of hydrogen storage technologies
Life cycle assessment (LCA) quantifies the environmental burdens of
hydrogen storage technologies across material extraction, production,
operation, and end-of-life stages [2]. Key environmental impacts identified include energy consumption, greenhouse gas emissions, resource
depletion, and potential pollution.
Compressed and liquid hydrogen storage methods require substantial energy for compression and liquefaction, which lead to significant
carbon footprints, particularly when electricity is sourced from fossil
fuels [5]. Moreover, the manufacturing of storage tanks and cryogenic equipment contributes to environmental impacts due to material
production and processing. Chemical storage methods, such as the
production of metal hydrides and complex hydrides, are also energyintensive and may involve hazardous chemicals, resulting in emissions
and waste [168]. The recycling and disposal of spent materials from
these processes pose additional environmental challenges. Advanced
nanostructured materials require specialized synthesis techniques and
chemicals, which contribute to energy consumption and potential environmental impacts; however, the improved efficiency and performance
of these materials may offset these impacts over their life cycle [169].
To mitigate the environmental impacts of hydrogen storage technologies, a multifaceted approach involving technological advancements, sustainable practices, and policy interventions is essential. Improving energy efficiency is paramount; developing more efficient storage technologies and processes can significantly reduce energy consumption. This includes innovations in compression technologies, liquefaction processes, and material synthesis to enhance overall efficiency [33]. Utilizing renewable energy sources for hydrogen production, compression, liquefaction, and regeneration processes substantially lowers the carbon footprint as integration with renewable
energy systems enhances sustainability [170]. Establishing comprehensive recycling systems for storage equipment materials is also crucial
in reducing waste and environmental pollution. Advances in recycling
technologies for composite materials, metals, and nanomaterials are essential to facilitate this process [6]. Adopting environmentally friendly
manufacturing practices, such as green chemistry principles and clean
production technologies, minimizes harmful emissions, making the
development of scalable, low-impact synthesis methods for storage
materials critical [21].
Implementing strict environmental policies and industry standards
helps regulate production, implementation, and waste disposal processes, while incentivizing the adoption of green technologies and
sustainable practices promotes industry-wide improvements [2]. Enhancing safety protocols and implementing advanced monitoring technologies are vital to prevent leaks and environmental contamination; in
underground storage scenarios, real-time monitoring of pressure, gas
composition, and subsurface conditions is imperative [171]. Finally,
investing in research and development to create new materials with

Cryogenic systems face additional issues from material stress due to

hydrides, MOFs, or LOHCs, hydrogen is chemically or physically bound,
reducing the risk of leak during storage. However, leaks can still occur
during charging or release. Impurities such as oxygen or water can

routine inspection, compatible materials, and containment layers for
compressed and cryogenic systems, and strict control of temperature
and contamination for material-based storage [28].
Emergency Protocols: Hydrogen’s wide flammability range (4%–75%
in air) and low ignition energy (≈0.02 mJ) [9] demand strict emergency
protocols for all storage systems, including reliable detection, personnel
training, clear response procedures, and coordination with local responders. Modern facilities use multiple detection tools such as catalytic
bead sensors for low concentrations and thermal conductivity detectors
for higher ones [180]. According to hydrogen safety panel guidance,
sensors should be placed near valves, ceilings, and escape routes [181],
linked to alarms and emergency shutdown systems. Regular drills, as
recommended by NFPA [182], are essential for readiness. Human error
accounts for about 60% of incidents [13], so staff must be prepared to
recognize hydrogen hazards, operate equipment properly, and respond
to leaks or fires. Given that hydrogen flames are nearly invisible
and emit little heat, firefighting protocols advise letting controlled
hydrogen fires burn unless the leak can be stopped to avoid explosion
risk [183]. Adequate ventilation-at least six air changes per hour
with sensor-triggered increases-is vital for indoor systems [184]. Postincident analysis should include root-cause investigation and sharing
of lessons learned, as recommended by DOE’s Safety Panel [185]. For
larger sites, joint planning and training with first responders ensure
effective external support and safe coordination during emergencies.
System Failures: Failures in hydrogen storage systems vary by
technology but often result from mechanical stress, thermal imbalance,
control faults, or material degradation. Compressed gas tanks can fail
due to fatigue, impact damage, or design flaws. Though composite
tanks undergo rigorous testing [186], analysis of 71 incidents revealed
that over-pressurization, poor maintenance, or incompatible materials
were often to blame [187]. Cryogenic systems are vulnerable to insulation loss, leading to rapid boil-off and pressure spikes. In hydride-based

[173]

systems, poor heat transfer during charging or discharging can reduce
performance or damage materials [99]. Effective thermal design and
monitoring are critical to avoid such failures.
Modern systems rely on sensors, valves, and logic controls to manage operation, but these can fail. One recorded incident involved
premature rupture disc activation due to control malfunction, which
safely vented the tank but emphasized the need for redundant safeguards [188]. Material embrittlement is another concern-hydrogen
diffusion weakens some metals, cutting fracture toughness by up to
90% [189]. Proper material selection and inspection are essential.
Impurities such as CO or moisture can also damage storage media or
poison catalysts [146]. Mitigation includes redundant pressure relief
devices, fail-safe system design, real-time monitoring, and strict adherence to safety standards like ISO 19881 and ASME Section VIII [186,
190]. A robust safety framework must combine technical safeguards
with training, oversight, and a strong safety culture.
8. Conclusion, research gaps, and future directions
Hydrogen storage technologies have advanced considerably in recent years, with significant progress across diverse material systems
and storage approaches. These advancements target longstanding challenges in efficiency, safety, and scalability, contributing to the broader
shift toward a reliable and sustainable hydrogen economy. This comprehensive study of hydrogen storage technologies reveals that while
significant progress has been made across multiple storage approaches,
each method presents distinct trade-offs that affects its suitability for
specific applications. Compressed gas storage offers technological maturity but requires high-pressure infrastructure, liquid hydrogen provides
high density but demands continuous energy input, physical adsorption methods show promise but typically need low temperatures, and
chemical storage approaches offer safety advantages but often require
elevated temperatures for hydrogen release. Recent breakthroughs in
formic acid systems, solid-state reticular materials, and photocatalytic
approaches that harness renewable energy demonstrate significant advancements. However, the fundamental challenge of developing storage technologies that operate efficiently under ambient conditions is
still persist.
The most critical research gap identified is the lack of ambientcondition hydrogen storage systems that achieve practical densities
without extreme operational requirements, particularly constraining
transportation and portable applications where normal operating conditions are essential. Immediate research priorities must focus on developing advanced materials with tailored thermodynamic properties

Advancing hydrogen storage technologies will require a strategic
