Enhancing absorption performance in metal hydride hydrogen storage:
Implementation of fins and a water jacket
Dae Yeob Lee , Yasser Mahmoudi , Vincenzo Spallina , Amir Keshmiri *
School of Engineering, The University of Manchester, Manchester, M13 9PL, UK

A R T I C L E I N F O

A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
CFD
Metal hydride
Hydrogen storage
Heat and mass transfer
Cooling jacket

This study investigates the flow and heat transfer characteristics of a Metal Hydride (MH) hydrogen storage
device featuring internal fins and a water jacket for improved hydrogen storage performance. The MH is
modelled as a porous medium, with computational fluid dynamics used to analyse transient heat and mass
transfer, incorporating a conjugate turbulent heat transfer model for the water jacket. Investigations covered fin
arrangements (in-line and staggered), materials (stainless-steel and copper), water flow rates (Re =
5100–22000), and hydrogen inlet pressures (5–20 bar). Results show that staggered copper fins and higher flow
rates or pressures significantly reduce hydrogen absorption time: staggered fins with Re = 5100 reduce ab­
sorption time by 30 %, Re = 22000 achieves further 27 % reduction, and increasing inlet pressure to 20 bar
decreased it by 61 %. However, increased fin numbers and pressures introduce challenges such as pressure drop
and reactor mass. Four reactor designs were assessed, highlighting trade-offs in performance.

1. Introduction
Hydrogen offers a high energy density, serves as a clean energy
source, and produces no harmful emissions [1]. In addition, given that
hydrogen is the most abundant element in the universe, it is a compel­
ling candidate for developing a new, suitable energy infrastructure [2].
However, hydrogen storage presents a significant challenge due to its
extremely low density (0.089 g/L) under atmospheric conditions [3].
Compared to other energy sources, hydrogen gas exhibits a notably low
volumetric energy density under these conditions, although it possesses
a high energy density per mass [3,4]. Consequently, various methods,
such as compression and liquefaction, are necessary to increase its
volumetric energy density. These conventional techniques are straight­
forward to increase hydrogen density, nevertheless, they also introduce
challenges related to safety and the substantial energy consumption
required for these processes [5,6].
Metal hydrides (MHs) for hydrogen storage are emerging as a novel
approach, offering high volumetric energy density and operation under

less severe conditions with enhanced safety [7]. MHs hydrogen storage
utilise chemical bonds formed between hydrogen and metal or metal
alloys. When hydrogen molecules reach the metal surface, they first
dissociate and then hydrogen atoms diffuse into the metal lattice [8].
Since hydrogen can be stored in its atomic state, MHs hydrogen storage
system can achieve a very high volumetric energy density. The mecha­
nism of hydrogen charging and discharging is provided by absorption
and desorption, respectively. The kinetics of MHs are closely related to
temperature and pressure, and the direction of these gas-solid in­
teractions is determined by the equilibrium pressure [9]. Absorption is
dominant when the hydrogen gas pressure is higher than the equilibrium
pressure, while desorption proceeds when the hydrogen gas pressure is
lower. The absorption is an exothermic process, hence favoured at low
temperature [10]. The combination of absorption and desorption makes
MHs suitable for thermal applications such as hydrogen compression,
thermal energy storage, and heat pumps [11–13].
Several MHs have been researched for hydrogen storage applica­
tions. The most favoured MHs are magnesium (Mg)-based hydrides, ti­
tanium (Ti)-based hydrides, and lanthanum (La)-based hydrides [9].
Mg-based hydrides offer high hydrogen storage capacity but they suf­
fer from poor kinetics and require high temperature and pressure for
operation [14]. Ti-based hydrides are attractive due to their abundance
and long-term stability, however, their high equilibrium pressure, acti­
vation difficulties, and low hydrogenation rate limit their applicability
[7]. Finally, La-based hydrides have a relatively low hydrogen storage
capacity (1.4 wt%), however, under ambient temperature and pressure,
they exhibit fast kinetics, and possess a high hydrogen volumetric ca­
pacity [15]. These favourable characteristics make La-based hydrides a
promising material for various applications, and they are the most
commonly utilised on an industrial scale [16]. Therefore, in this study,
Lanthanum–Nickel alloy (LaNi5 ) was used to investigate the absorption
performance.
Overall, the storage capacity and design of appropriate equipment
require substantial effort to deal with slow absorption and desorption
due to the low thermal conductivity of MHs [17]. To address this issue,
many studies have explored effective heat transfer methods to enhance
MHs reaction rate through experiments and numerical simulations. The
most fundamental approaches include installing fins to enhance heat
transfer and using heat transfer fluids (HTF) in the reactor to increase
the cooling and heating. MacDonald and Rowe [18] applied external fins
to the reactor to reduce its thermal resistance, which effectively
decreased the hydrogen desorption time. Askri et al. [19] compared the
effects of internal and external fins on absorption time in an MH reactor
using a mathematical model. Chung et al. [20] experimentally assessed
the absorption and desorption time of an MH reactor using a central heat
pipe with external fins. Both absorption and desorption times were
reduced by half compared to the case without heat pipe. Chibani et al.
[21] applied external fins with phase change materials in the reactor as a
cooling method for large-scale hydrogen storage within an energy
storage system. Bai et al. [22] optimised the absorption time and ther­
mal resistance of MH reactor by implementing an internal longitudinal
tree-shaped fin design, achieving a 21 % reduction in absorption time
compared to simple radial fins. The installation of an HTF tube has
generally demonstrated a substantial reduction in absorption time. Tong
et al. [23] developed a reactor design incorporating various types of heat
exchanger pipes, including straight and coiled-tube configurations.
Although the presence of the heat exchanger reduces the quantity of
stored hydrogen, it significantly decreases hydrogen absorption time by
34 % and 73 %, respectively. Parida and Muthukumar [24] developed
an MH absorption bed with fins and a central cooling tube. Three fin
designs, which is longitudinal, transverse, and spiral fins are compared
for absorption and desorption rate. A few studies have analysed MH
reactors with internal cooling fins and an external water jacket, con­
ducting parametric studies on operating conditions, fins designs, and
different cooling fluids [25,26].
Similar research was conducted by Prasad and Muthukumar [27].

They compared two cases: 1) outer cooling channel, and 2) outer cooling
channel with internal fins. The results of the numerical analysis showed
that the second case exhibited a 51.8 % improvement in absorption time,
and the peak temperature of the thermal fluid in case 1 was 35.6 ◦ C,
while in case 2, it was 41.2 ◦ C, demonstrating the effective heat transfer
achieved using internal fins.
Although several studies have considered internal and external HTF
flow for effective cooling, there is still lack of some fundamental aspect
of the MH modelling, as outlined below:
(i) Previous studies have not incorporated a fully coupled conjugate
heat transfer model for MH reaction, where heat transfer in both
solid and fluid domain is simultaneously solved. This modelling
approach is essential because the heat and mass transfer during
absorption strongly depend on local temperature, hydrogen
concentration, and pressure [28]. By direct coupling heat transfer
between solid structures and surrounding fluid flow, the present
study provides a more realistic and detailed representation of
transient behaviour during hydrogen absorption.
(ii) Most of the previous studies have simplified the cooling system
by applying a fixed heat transfer coefficient (HTC) instead of
simulating the actual cooling fluid flow. The present study con­
ducts a transient simulation incorporating a turbulent cooling
flow, allowing for a more accurate assessment of its impact on
MH absorption performance. Furthermore, instead of assuming a
constant HTC, HTC values are inversely derived under different
conditions.
(iii) Due to MH expansion during absorption, the simulation should
include an interface between MH bed and the expansion vol­
ume–a domain of pure hydrogen gas–to secure space for expan­
sion (Fig. 1). Moreover, incorporating the expansion zone affects
the thermal flow pattern and increases thermal resistance, lead­
ing to reduced reaction rates [29].
The present study aims to address these gaps by developing a
comprehensive numerical model that incorporates conjugate heat
transfer and turbulence model for HTF flow in the reactor. In this paper,
MH absorption for hydrogen charging was investigated using compu­
tational fluid dynamics (CFD). The established simulation model was

2. Problem definition
2.1. Reactor design
To investigate the cooling effect of internal fins and water jacket,
four different reactor configurations are compared. The geometry of the
reactor is composed of four zones: the MH bed, the expansion zone, the
reactor wall with fins, and water jacket. The MH bed is a porous medium
for LaNi5 particles. The LaNi5 alloy undergoes approximately 17 %
volumetric expansion during absorption [30]. To prevent deformation
of the reactor due to the stress generated in this process, an adequate
amount of space is required, which is designed as the expansion zone. In
the present reactor designs, 19 % of expansion volume is secured to
prevent reactor deformation.
The reactor is cylindrical in shape, with water entering from the top
of the reactor, flowing down to the bottom, and then rising to the top rim

4.5. Application of copper fin
In this section, the impact of using copper fins instead of stainlesssteel fins is analysed, while the reactor itself remains made of
stainless-steel. Copper has a thermal conductivity that is approximately
20 times higher than stainless-steel, making widely used material for
enhancing heat transfer. As a result, the use of copper fins is expected to
significantly improve heat transfer efficiency in the reactor. For a
comprehensive comparison between stainless-steel and copper fins,
hydrogen charging times for different reactor designs and water flow Re
evaluated. The overall comparison between stainless-steel and copper
fins is presented in Fig. 11.
The overall hydrogen charging time is notably reduced across all
reactor designs and water flow rates when copper fins are used. In the
previous results from Section 4.2 Reactor Design, it was showed that
stainless-steel fins reduced hydrogen charging time by a maximum of 14
% when comparing Design 1 to Design 4. However, in the same case,
copper fins reduced the charging time by 38 %. The effectiveness of
copper fins is especially apparent when the surface area of fins is

high thermal conductivity materials, such as copper, inside the MH bed,
along with even a slight increase in surface area, is highly effective in
reducing hydrogen charging time.
4.6. Optimisation of reactor design
The presence of porous medium (i.e. MH), internal fins, and their
arrangements affects the pressure drop in MH bed. This is because the
fins act as obstacles, reducing the available hydrogen flow area. Addi­
tionally, the increased surface area in the MH bed increases frictional
drag. A high pressure drop requires more power to compress the MH
bed, and the resulting lower pressure and hydrogen velocity in MH bed
can reduce the hydrogen absorption rate. Therefore, selecting the
optimal reactor design must carefully consider this trade-off. Fig. 12 (a)
presents the hydrogen charging times and corresponding pressure drop
for each reactor design. For the comparison, the copper fin with water
flow rate in Re 22000 is applied. The pressure drop is measured from the
interface of the expansion zone and the MH bed to the bottom of the MH
bed, with an inlet hydrogen pressure of 10 bar for all designs. The
pressure drop for reactor designs 1 to 4 are 350, 359, 368, and 372 Pa,
respectively. As the number of fins increases, a higher pressure drop is
observed. In the case of staggered fins (Design 4), hydrogen movement
within the MH bed becomes more restricted, leading to a further in­
crease in pressure drop. The corresponding pressure drop is increased to
3 %, 5 %, and 6 % for Design 2, 3, and 4, respectively. On the other hand,
the hydrogen charging time is significantly reduced in these designs,
decreasing to 83 %, 68 %, and 62 % of the baseline (Design 1). Although
pressure drop occur in all designs, the magnitude is approximately 0.04
% of the inlet pressure, making its impact on compression power or
reaction rate negligible.

reactor mass. The mass increases observed in Design 2, 3 and 4 are 1.6
%, 3.1 %, and 4 %, respectively, compared to Design 1. All reactor de­

5. Conclusion
This study conducted numerical simulations of transient heat and
mass transfer during MH absorption, focusing on overcoming the chal­
lenges posed by the low thermal conductivity of LaNi5 alloy through
reactor design enhancement. The cylindrical reactors with internal
cooling fins and an outer water jacket were tested under various
boundary conditions to evaluate their performance. Four reactor con­
figurations were studied: 0, 5, and 10 in-line fins and 10 staggered ar­
rangements. The main findings of this study are as follows:

This study provides insights into how reactor design modifications,
specifically cooling enhancements, can reduce the absorption time of
MH. Based on these findings, the proposed reactor design (Design 4
incorporating copper fins) offers the best performance. Future work will
focus on experimental studies of metal hydride absorption and desorp­
tion using novel heat exchanger designs especially unique fin geometries
with a combination of different MH under a broad range of operation
parameters. By integrating computational and experimental ap­
proaches, our research will provide a more robust understanding of MH
hydrogen storage systems, bridging the gap between theoretical and
practical implementation. Finally, utilising different machine learning
algorithms such as Physics Informed Neural Network (PINN) [42,43]
towards optimisation of the reactor/fin design could be utilised.

• The application of 10 internal cooling fins in the MH reactor reduced
the time to charge 90 % of maximum capacity by 14.6 %. Moreover,
using a staggered arrangement of fins further reduced the charging
time by 3.2 % compared to the inline arrangement. MH bed tem­
perature and hydrogen charging profiles indicated that cooling fins
significantly improved heat dissipation during absorption,
enhancing the reaction rate.
