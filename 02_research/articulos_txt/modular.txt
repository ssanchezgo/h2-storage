Introduction
Greenhouse gas emissions from carbon dioxide (CO2),
methane (CH4), and other substances are largely a result of
fossil fuel use, and contribute greatly to global warming and
climate change. In Japan, almost 40% of the total emitted CO2
is due to energy consumption within buildings, which greatly
exceeds the contributions from mobility sources (~16%) [1]. In
December 2017, the Japanese government released a “Basic
Hydrogen Strategy” [2], which provides a roadmap for the

realization of a hydrogen-based society (this roadmap was
revised March 12, 2019 [3]). Included in the roadmap are zeroemission buildings (ZEBs), which have an effective annual
energy consumption of zero through energy conservation and
renewable energy (RE) power generation [4]. Accordingly,
reducing CO2 emissions from buildings is an absolute
necessity.
In many cases, stationary hydrogen energy utilization
systems with RE such as photovoltaic (PV) panels and wind
turbines consist of an electrolyzer (Ely) for hydrogen

production, as well as hydrogen storage tanks and fuel cells
(FC) for hydrogen use [5e11]. Batteries for short-term energy
storage are often included in the stationary system [12]. To
achieve the ZEB, we have developed a bench-scale hydrogen
energy system with RE [13]; simulation results for the system
design and its operations are detailed in our other work [14]. In
that system, 20-kW PV panels are used for RE power generation because they can be easily installed near or on buildings.
Our hydrogen system consists of Ely (5 Nm3/h), metal hydride
(MH) tanks (80 Nm3), FC (3.5 kW), and batteries (20 kW/
20 kWh). This bench-scale system was aimed to achieve a ZEB
for a small low-rise building (total floor area ~1000 m2; demand: ~5 kW) [14].
To use hydrogen in urban environments (i.e., strict regulation areas), our stationary system prioritizes compactness,
safety, and mild operation conditions (e.g. < 1 MPa and 70  C).
We thus selected MH tanks that can store hydrogen compactly
and operate near ambient conditions [15e18]. The MH in the
tanks is not classified as a hazardous material under the Fire
Service Act of Japan. We suggest that MH hydrogen storage
(MHHS) is the least regulated storage method and thus suitable for use in urban areas. The maximum hydrogen pressure
is controlled such that it is less than 1 MPa (Gauge), to adhere
to the High Pressure Gas Safety Regulation of Japan [19].
AB5-type rare-earth alloys (e.g., LaNi5 and Mm(NiCoAl)5)
are the most typical hydrogen storage alloys and have been
used as bench-scale MHHS for stationary systems
[5e11,20e22], because they possess easy activation, small
hysteresis, and rapid kinetics. However, AB5 alloys include
expensive rare-earth metals and become pulverized after a
few ab/desorption cycles [23]. The pulverized alloy grains are
very small (~1 mm) and active, and are classified as hazardous
materials in Japan. Therefore, MHHS units using the hazardous material must be placed at a safe distance (several meters)
from the building, meaning that the required space is often
too large for use in urban building areas [24].
We focused on the AB-type TiFe-based alloy [25] because of
the low cost of the materials and abundance of resources.
Moreover, many of the AB-type alloys have high pulverization
resistance and thus are not classified as hazardous materials.
The disadvantages of the alloys include large hysteresis, second plateau, and difficult activation treatments [22,23,25,26].
However, we investigated the activation processes of a benchscale MH tank filled with the TiFe-based alloy near ambient
conditions [27], and succeeded in developing the alloy with a
small second plateau and easy activation. One of the unique
features of our hydrogen energy utilization system is that the
alloy grains (total: 520 kg) were placed in each tank (TiFebased tank).
The second feature of our system is thermal integration
between the FC and TiFe-based tanks. Owing to the endothermic hydrogen desorption of the alloy, it is necessary to
heat the alloy bed for continuous hydrogen release. As the FC
simultaneously generates power and heat, desorption of the
TiFe-based tanks can be operated by using the FC heat output.
Many thermal integrations of FC and MH tanks have been
studied, and the MH tanks were commonly filled with AB5
alloys (AB5 tanks) [28e33]. However, few studies have investigated integrating FC and TiFe-based tanks. In particular, no
studies to our knowledge have examined bench-scale

14597

integrated systems, of which our integrated system would be
the largest scale (3.5 kW and 520 kg).
The third feature of our hydrogen system is that it can be
operated automatically by a building energy management
system (BEMS) that was developed by Shimizu Co. called
“Smart BEMS” [34]. Smart BEMS plans energy management
based on weather and demand forecasts and minimizes grid
power consumption (⇔ the CO2 emissions). Based on the
excess PV power and the power demand of the building, the
hydrogen apparatuses and charge-discharge of the batteries
can be controlled by the Smart BEMS.
In this paper, a bench-scale hydrogen energy utilization
system was constructed to realize a ZEB. Our system was
aimed to be employed in a small-rise building to experimentally realize a ZEB. We describe the specifications of each
system component and detail the technical aspects that save
cost/space in the system design. Subsequently, we present
experimental results from 24-h operation in fine weather
conditions. Details on the thermal integration of the FC and
TiFe-based tanks are also presented. We believe our results
will help enable practical applications of the stationary
hydrogen system.

Hydrogen use－Fuel cells
Hydrogen production-electrolyzer
For the system to be employed in urban areas, compactness
is a high priority. Hydrogen is produced by excess PV power,
so the apparatus must be suitable for daily start/stop (DSS)
and partial load (PL) operations. Thus, we selected a
compact polymer electrolyte membrane (PEM) Ely (Hitachi
Zosen, HYDROSPRING™) and pure water, instead of an
alkaline Ely that occupies a larger space and has a
poisonous solution. The PEM Ely is capable of hydrogen
production of 5 Nm3/h with DC power of 26 kW. Maximum
hydrogen supply pressure to the TiFe-based tanks is
0.97 MPaG to conform with the Japan High Pressure Gas
Safety Act [19].
Fig. 2 shows a photograph of the PEM Ely installed in the
container. The cell stacks consist of 26 electrochemical cells.
Other specifications include a radiator (Mitsubishi Electric,
EWF-50FTA) used for the cell stacks cooling to save space and
cost, and no cooling tower, allowing for easy relocation. Two
dehumidifiers (Japan Pionics, JHP-10E-S) are used for dehumidification of hydrogen from the PEM Ely. The dew point of
the hydrogen supplied to the TiFe-based tanks is less than
50  C. To reduce hydrogen emissions outside the container, a
gas tank stores hydrogen at a high dew point (see H2 tank in
Fig. 2), using the under-regeneration operation of a dehumidifier. The air valve compressor is the same as that used in
the TiFe-based tanks, to save space and cost. Further, in this
system, electrolysis waste heat is used for heat storage and
heating the MH tanks.
Fig. 3 shows the start-up of the PEM Ely. The run-up operation begins about 2 min after the start, followed by rated
operation (5 Nm3/h) approximately 5 min after the start. The
Ely has a fast start-up, which indicates that it is suitable for
the DSS operation. Furthermore, the fast start-up is not
dependent on the cell stacks’ temperature.

Fig. 7 shows the start-up of the PEM FC. The run-up operation (1.25 kW) starts approximately 7 min after startup, and
the rated-operation (3.5 kW) approximately 10 min after
startup. This result demonstrates that the PEM FC has a fast

Fig. 9 shows pressure-composition isotherm (PCI) properties of the TiFe-based alloy used to fill the tanks. As mentioned
above, many TiFe-based alloys have large hysteresis and a
second plateau, which are generally disadvantages
[22,23,25,26]. Although the hysteresis remained, the second
plateau was smaller than a binary alloy [25] (see Fig. 9). The
hydrogen supply pressure from the Ely to the TiFe-based tanks
is 0.97 MPa. Meanwhile, the hydrogen pressure of the FC
operation has to be less than 50 kPaG (minimum pressure of
15 kPaG); as such, the hydrogen supply pressure from the
TiFe-based tanks to the FC was lowered by the pressure
reducer. Therefore, we only had to set the hydrogen pressure
of the TiFe-based tanks below 0.97 MPaG and over 15 kPaG
under the hydrogen production (Ely þ MH) and utilization
(MH þ FC) operations, respectively. Overall, the hysteresis and
second plateau of the developed alloy are not major disadvantages; in reality, the TiFe-based tanks were able to be
operated similarly to the AB5 tanks. This is a very important
point for MHHS using the TiFe-based tanks.

Results and discussion
24-h operation in fine weather conditions
A 24-h operation was carried out automatically by the Smart
BEMS [34]. Each system component was controlled by the
Smart BEMS to maximally reduce grid power consumption.
Based on a weather forecast from the previous day, the Smart
BEMS planned the operation schedule for every 30 min up to
48 h ahead, then modified the operation schedule based on
updated weather forecasts at 7:00, 12:00, and 19:00 on the
operating day. The Smart BEMS was able to maintain realtime control within several seconds; that is, the Smart BEMS
specified when the batteries charged and discharged for
supply-demand balance based on PV power and building demand. The details of the control logics have been reported in
our other work [14].
Fig. 10 shows the energy flow of the hydrogen system with
PV. The direct-use PV power has the highest priority. In

addition, when excess PV power is generated, the batteries
operate for short-term/small quantity energy storage. Finally,
power-to-hydrogen was selected for long-term/large quantity
energy storage. The FC power supply was designed for longterm operations under night or cloudy/rain conditions. In
this experiment, we consider the power consumption of the
control room and the auxiliaries’ power as building demand
(~5 kW), which is close to that of our target low-rise building.
Fig. 11 shows the 24-h operation results under fine weather
conditions. The left vertical axis indicates the power supply
(upper) and the demand/energy storage (lower). The gray line
shows the energy storage rate (hydrogen þ battery) on the
right vertical axis. Although the operation was controlled
every 30 min, the time variations are shown every 1 h because
there was almost no difference in the results between the two
time intervals. The building demand (blue) was supplied by
the FC power (dark blue) from 0:00 to 7:00. Because the demand was smaller than the PV power output, excess PV power
(green) was generated after 8:00 and the batteries charged the
excess PV power at 8:00 (orange). Subsequently, hydrogen
production (light blue) occurred from 9:00 to 16:00, during
which period the charge-discharge operations of the Li-ion
batteries were due to the real-time control. The energy storage rate (gray line) depended mainly on the hydrogen storage
amount because the difference between the charge and
discharge of the batteries was small. On account of little grid
power (red), the planned operation was successfully carried
out; that is, ZEB was achieved.

Thermal integration of PEM FC－Metal hydride tanks
The thermal integration between the PEM FC and TiFe-based
tanks was demonstrated. Integrated systems have been reported [28e33], with many using AB5-type alloys and others
using AB2-type alloys [33,38e40]. However, few studies have
used TiFe-based tanks. To the best of our knowledge, our
integration scale is the largest with 3.5 kW power and a 520 kg
alloy.
MH Tank 1 was used in this experiment (see Figs. 6 and 8);
the results are shown in Fig. 12. The initial temperature,
pressure, and hydrogen storage of the tanks were 30  C,
0.15 MPa, and 20 Nm3 (H/M ¼ 0.5), respectively. Here, the
temperature of the tanks refers to that of the alloy bed. The FC
power output and hydrogen flow rate were 3.5 kW and

2.1 Nm3/h, respectively. The FC outlet water temperature
increased up to 60  C in 20 min after the power output started,
and then held steady at 60  C. The heat exchange between FC
and the heat transfer liquid was carried out at the heat
exchanger indicated in Fig. 6(a), after which the heat was
supplied to the alloy tanks. The heat supply temperature to
the tanks was constant at approximately 42  C when the FC
outlet water temperature was 60  C. The flow rate of the heat
transfer liquid was 9.0 L/min.
As shown in Fig. 12, the temperature of the tanks increased
at 20 min after the FC power output, and pressure increased
accordingly. The temperature increased to 42  C after 3 h of
operation, and temperature and pressure subsequently
decreased gradually. Although the hydrogen flow rate
(2.1 Nm3/h) and heat supply temperature (42  C) were constant during the operation, the tank temperature decreased.
This is because the thermal conductivity of the alloy bed
decreased with decreasing hydrogen storage amount; the
alloy grains contracted as the alloy desorbed hydrogen, and
the void fraction of the alloy bed increased [27,41]. For this
reason, the thermal conductivity decreased and the heat exchange between the alloy bed and the heat transfer liquid also
decreased. Despite the decreasing temperature and pressure
of the TiFe-based tanks, full desorption was carried out using
the FC heat output (pink line in Fig. 12).

Conclusions
We constructed a bench-scale hydrogen energy utilization
system with RE for a ZEB, with the aim of employing it in a
small low-rise building. Because of practical considerations in
urban areas, the priorities of our hydrogen system were
compactness, safety, and mild operation conditions. Consequently, PEM Ely and FC, MH tanks, and Li-ion batteries were
selected. Each system component was installed in a 12 ft
container that could be moved easily into any building area,
and was controlled automatically by the BEMS. Both PEM Ely
and FC exhibited fast start-up and high efficiency under partial load operations. We selected a TiFe-based alloy for MH
hydrogen storage because of metal cost and Japanese regulations. The tanks (TiFe-based tanks) were filled with 520 kg of
alloy grains. A 24-h operation on a fine day was carried out by
the BEMS, and results showed that there was nearly zero grid
power consumption, i.e. ZEB was realized. The thermal integration of PEM FC and TiFe-based tanks showed that full
desorption could be completed through partial use of FC heat
output. Our results are relevant to the practical application of
stationary hydrogen energy systems in urban building areas.
